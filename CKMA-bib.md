# Core Knowledge in Mathematical Analysis

## A graduate and research bibliography

This is a working bibliography of the books that form the common
language of modern mathematical analysis. It is not a ranking, and it
is not complete; completeness in a subject this large is not a
coherent goal. The selection favours four kinds of book:

1. **graduate standards** that give a durable first or second course;
2. **research monographs** that established the language and the
   methods of a subject;
3. **large references** that analysts actually consult for a precise
   theorem, a variant hypothesis, or bibliographic orientation;
4. **problem books, tables, and handbooks** that are used rather than
   read.

The organising principle is that a book belongs here if knowing that
it exists, and roughly what role it plays, is part of the working
culture of graduate or research-level analysis. That criterion admits
some books nobody reads cover to cover, and excludes some excellent
books that never became common reference points.

### Conventions

Within each heading, books that serve as entry points generally come
first, followed by standard references, followed by research
monographs. A title that genuinely belongs in three places is listed
in three places, with the annotation adjusted each time; cross-listing
is deliberate, since the point of the list is navigation rather than
taxonomy. Multi-volume works are cited as a unit unless the volumes
are usually consulted separately.

Editions and publishers are omitted. Nearly everything here exists in
several printings, many of the older items are now freely and legally
available, and edition numbers date faster than the mathematics does.

# Part I. Panoramas, History, and Foundations

---

## 1. Broad Courses, Encyclopedias, and Maps of Analysis

Books that help locate a subject within analysis as a whole, or that
are consulted across many subjects.

* **Barry Simon, *A Comprehensive Course in Analysis*** (5 volumes:
  *Real Analysis*; *Basic Complex Analysis*; *Advanced Complex
  Analysis*; *Harmonic Analysis*; *Operator Theory*). A modern
  graduate-to-research panorama, unusually rich in problems,
  historical notes, and cross-connections; the closest thing the
  subject has to a single-author survey of itself.
* **Elias M. Stein and Rami Shakarchi, *Princeton Lectures in
  Analysis*** (4 volumes: *Fourier Analysis*; *Complex Analysis*;
  *Real Analysis*; *Functional Analysis*). The most coherent compact
  series for seeing Fourier, complex, real, and functional analysis as
  one subject rather than four courses.
* **Walter Rudin, *Real and Complex Analysis*** and ***Functional
  Analysis***. Compact rather than encyclopedic in size, but
  encyclopedic in influence; both remain models of terse graduate
  exposition, and both are still the implicit standard against which
  later texts are measured.
* **Lars Hörmander, *The Analysis of Linear Partial Differential
  Operators I–IV***. The definitive large-scale reference for
  distributions, Fourier analysis, pseudodifferential operators,
  propagation of singularities, and Fourier integral operators.
* **Michael E. Taylor, *Partial Differential Equations I–III***. A
  broad analyst's PDE course combining classical equations,
  pseudodifferential methods, geometry, spectral theory, and nonlinear
  problems; effectively a second graduate education in analysis.
* **Michael Reed and Barry Simon, *Methods of Modern Mathematical
  Physics I–IV***. Functional analysis, self-adjointness, scattering,
  and spectral analysis; still the main bridge from abstract analysis
  to Schrödinger operators.
* **Nelson Dunford and Jacob T. Schwartz, *Linear Operators
  I–III***. The great mid-century encyclopedia of Banach-space
  operator theory and spectral theory; archaic in notation, still
  technically valuable, and still occasionally the only place a
  particular theorem is stated in the generality one needs.
* **Nicolas Bourbaki, *Integration*** and ***Topological Vector
  Spaces***. Not classroom texts, but lasting sources for the
  structural language of measure, integration, locally convex spaces,
  and duality.
* **Jean Dieudonné, *Treatise on Analysis*** (7 volumes). The
  Bourbaki-school comprehensive course, from integration and manifolds
  through Lie groups, harmonic analysis, and linear functional
  equations; the modern successor to the classical *Cours d'analyse*.
* **Edwin Hewitt and Kenneth A. Ross, *Abstract Harmonic Analysis
  I–II***. The standard large reference for locally compact groups,
  Haar measure, representation theory, and Pontryagin duality.
* **Antoni Zygmund, *Trigonometric Series*** (2 volumes). The
  classical encyclopedia of Fourier series and an enduring source for
  sharp convergence and summability results.
* **Einar Hille and Ralph S. Phillips, *Functional Analysis and
  Semi-Groups***. Historically foundational and still useful for the
  operator-semigroup point of view and for its unusually wide
  classical coverage.
* **Richard Courant and David Hilbert, *Methods of Mathematical
  Physics*** (2 volumes). Classical analysis, variational methods,
  eigenvalue problems, and PDE in a style that still repays
  consultation.
* **Vladimir I. Smirnov, *A Course of Higher Mathematics*** (5 volumes
  in many parts). The great Russian comprehensive course; uneven by
  modern standards but remarkably wide, and still a first place to
  look for classical computations.
* **Timothy Gowers, June Barrow-Green, and Imre Leader (eds.), *The
  Princeton Companion to Mathematics***. Not a technical reference,
  but the best short orientation to how the analytic subjects relate
  to each other and to the rest of mathematics.
* **Springer *Encyclopaedia of Mathematical Sciences*** (the
  *Commutative Harmonic Analysis*, *Several Complex Variables*,
  *Dynamical Systems*, and *Partial Differential Equations*
  series). Survey volumes by leading Russian-school authors; variable
  in depth but often the fastest orientation to an unfamiliar area.
* **Reinhold Remmert and others, *Numbers*** and the Springer
  *Readings in Mathematics* tradition. Worth knowing for conceptual
  and historical framing of the objects analysis takes for granted.

## 2. History, Perspective, and Essays

Analysis is unusually shaped by its own history: notation,
definitions, and the boundaries between subfields are historical
artefacts, and knowing why they are where they are saves time.

* **Jean Dieudonné, *History of Functional Analysis***. The standard
  account of how the subject assembled itself between Fredholm and
  Grothendieck.
* **Thomas Hawkins, *Lebesgue's Theory of Integration: Its Origins and
  Development***. Why measure theory looks the way it does; still the
  best book on the emergence of modern integration.
* **Albrecht Pietsch, *History of Banach Spaces and Linear
  Operators***. Encyclopedic, opinionated, and full of attributions
  unavailable elsewhere.
* **Umberto Bottazzini, *The Higher Calculus: A History of Real and
  Complex Analysis from Euler to Weierstrass***. The
  nineteenth-century background to almost everything in Parts II and
  VII.
* **Jean Dieudonné, *A History of Algebraic and Differential
  Topology*** and ***Foundations of Modern Analysis***. The latter is
  a course, not a history, but its austere viewpoint is itself a
  historical document of the Bourbaki influence on analysis teaching.
* **Antonie F. Monna, *Functional Analysis in Historical
  Perspective***. Shorter and more idiosyncratic than Dieudonné, with
  useful coverage of the Dutch and French schools.
* **Constantin Carathéodory, *Vorlesungen über reelle Funktionen***. A
  historical source that is also still a usable treatise on real
  functions and measure.
* **Jean-Pierre Kahane and Pierre Gilles Lemarié-Rieusset, *Fourier
  Series and Wavelets***. A history-conscious account of how classical
  Fourier analysis turned into wavelet theory.
* **Reinhold Remmert, *Theory of Complex Functions*** and ***Classical
  Topics in Complex Function Theory***. Listed again in Part VII, but
  they belong here too: among the few graduate texts that treat the
  historical record as mathematically informative.
* **Vladimir Maz'ya and Tatyana Shaposhnikova, *Jacques Hadamard: A
  Universal Mathematician***. A scientific biography that doubles as a
  history of French analysis.
* **Barry Simon's historical notes**, scattered through *A
  Comprehensive Course in Analysis* and *Szegő's Theorem and Its
  Descendants*. Collectively one of the most reliable modern sources
  for attribution in analysis.

## 3. Set-Theoretic and Descriptive Foundations for Analysts

Polish spaces, Borel and analytic sets, and measurable selection are
part of the working equipment of measure theory, probability, ergodic
theory, and operator algebras.

* **Alexander S. Kechris, *Classical Descriptive Set Theory***. The
  standard modern reference: Polish spaces, Borel hierarchies,
  analytic and coanalytic sets, and Borel equivalence relations.
* **S. M. Srivastava, *A Course on Borel Sets***. A gentler and more
  self-contained route to the same material, with measurable selection
  treated carefully.
* **Yiannis N. Moschovakis, *Descriptive Set Theory***. The deeper
  treatment, including the effective theory and determinacy.
* **Howard Becker and Alexander S. Kechris, *The Descriptive Set
  Theory of Polish Group Actions***. The foundation of the modern
  theory of classification problems, with direct consequences in
  ergodic theory and operator algebras.
* **Kazimierz Kuratowski, *Topology I–II***. The classical source for
  Polish spaces, Baire category, and the topological facts measure
  theory quietly assumes.
* **Ryszard Engelking, *General Topology***. The standard reference
  when a point-set question actually needs a careful answer.
* **John L. Kelley, *General Topology***. Older and shorter, with the
  nets-and-filters language that functional analysis uses.
* **D. H. Fremlin, *Measure Theory*, volume 5 (*Set-theoretic Measure
  Theory*)**. Where measure theory meets independence results,
  measurable cardinals, and the limits of what is provable.
* **Thomas Jech, *Set Theory***. The reference for when an analytic
  question turns out to be independent of ZFC, which happens more
  often than the folklore suggests.
* **Su Gao, *Invariant Descriptive Set Theory***. Borel reducibility
  and classification, now a standard tool in the structure theory of
  operator algebras and dynamics.
* **Vladimir Kanovei, *Borel Equivalence Relations***. A
  research-level complement on the structure of classification
  problems.

# Part II. Real Analysis, Measure, and Probability

## 4. Real Analysis, Measure, and Integration

### Graduate foundations

* **Gerald B. Folland, *Real Analysis: Modern Techniques and Their
  Applications***. The standard compact American graduate text:
  measure, integration, product measures, differentiation, Banach and
  Hilbert spaces, and a first Fourier theory.
* **Walter Rudin, *Real and Complex Analysis***. A faster, more
  compressed route through Lebesgue integration, complex measures,
  functional analysis, Hardy spaces, and Haar measure, with the
  complex theory interleaved rather than segregated.
* **Halsey L. Royden and Patrick Fitzpatrick, *Real Analysis***. More
  leisurely than Folland and well suited to a first graduate course.
* **Terence Tao, *An Introduction to Measure Theory***. Discursive,
  exercise-rich, and particularly good for self-study.
* **Elias M. Stein and Rami Shakarchi, *Real Analysis: Measure Theory,
  Integration, and Hilbert Spaces***. The measure-theory volume of the
  Princeton series, notable for treating differentiation and Hausdorff
  measure early.
* **John B. Conway, *A Course in Abstract Analysis***. Measure and
  functional analysis developed together in a unified modern style.
* **Karl R. Stromberg, *An Introduction to Classical Real
  Analysis***. Old-fashioned in the best sense: careful, concrete, and
  strong on classical real-variable technique.
* **Robert G. Bartle, *The Elements of Integration and Lebesgue
  Measure***. Short, precise, and useful as a supplement rather than a
  main text.
* **Paul R. Halmos, *Measure Theory***. The classical abstract
  treatment; still outstanding on measure algebras, regularity, and
  structural organization.
* **Donald L. Cohn, *Measure Theory***. A clear modern reference,
  especially useful for regular Borel measures on locally compact
  spaces.
* **Edwin Hewitt and Karl Stromberg, *Real and Abstract
  Analysis***. Large, careful, and stronger than most first courses on
  topological and abstract measure theory.
* **Frank Jones, *Lebesgue Integration on Euclidean Space***. Concrete
  and geometric, and unusually good on the Euclidean specifics that
  abstract treatments skip.
* **Michael E. Taylor, *Measure Theory and Integration***. A compact
  graduate account by an author who knows exactly which parts get used
  later.

### Large references and advanced measure theory

* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Measure Theory*** (2 volumes). The
  contemporary encyclopedia: Radon measures, measurable mappings, weak
  convergence, capacities, topological measure theory, and a
  bibliography of extraordinary depth.
* **D. H. Fremlin, *Measure Theory*** (5 volumes). An enormous
  systematic reference running from elementary measure theory to
  measure algebras, topological measure theory, and set-theoretic
  independence; freely available and unmatched in completeness.
* **Nicolas Bourbaki, *Integration***. The structural account of
  integration on locally compact spaces, vector integration, and Haar
  measure.
* **K. R. Parthasarathy, *Probability Measures on Metric
  Spaces***. Tightness, Prokhorov compactness, and the
  measure-theoretic foundations of weak convergence.
* **V. S. Varadarajan, *Measures on Topological Spaces***. A concise
  reference for Radon measures and topological measure theory.
* **J. Diestel and J. J. Uhl, Jr., *Vector Measures***. Vector
  integration, Radon–Nikodým phenomena, compactness, and the
  interaction with Banach-space geometry.
* **Joe Diestel and Angela Spalsbury, *The Joys of Haar Measure***. A
  modern route to invariant measure and its links with harmonic
  analysis.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Differentiable Measures and the Malliavin
  Calculus***. Differentiability of measures on infinite-dimensional
  spaces, and the measure-theoretic background to stochastic analysis.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Gaussian Measures***. The standard
  monograph on Gaussian measures in infinite dimensions.
* **Nik Weaver, *Measure Theory and Functional Analysis***. Short,
  modern, and attentive to the functional-analytic reformulation of
  measure.
* **Alexandra Bellow and others (eds.), the *Measure Theory
  Oberwolfach* volumes**. Useful when tracking down a specialized
  result in vector or topological measure theory.

## 5. Differentiation and the Fine Structure of Real Functions

* **Andrew M. Bruckner, Judith B. Bruckner, and Brian S. Thomson,
  *Real Analysis***. An unusually rich treatment of derivatives, Baire
  classes, and the fine structure of real functions; the best modern
  home for the material Saks made classical.
* **Stanisław Saks, *Theory of the Integral***. The classical
  monograph on differentiation theory, Denjoy–Perron integration, and
  descriptive real analysis.
* **Lawrence C. Evans and Ronald F. Gariepy, *Measure Theory and Fine
  Properties of Functions***. The modern working reference for
  differentiation of measures, Sobolev and BV functions, Hausdorff
  measure, and approximate continuity; short enough to read and
  precise enough to cite.
* **Herbert Federer, *Geometric Measure Theory***. Far beyond a first
  course, but one of the ultimate sources for differentiation,
  densities, Hausdorff measures, and rectifiability.
* **Brian S. Thomson, *Real Functions*** and ***Differentiation of
  Integrals in R^n*** (with Guzmán's monograph below). Specialized but
  definitive on covering theorems and derivation bases.
* **Miguel de Guzmán, *Differentiation of Integrals in R^n***. The
  standard monograph on covering arguments, maximal functions, and the
  failure of differentiation for general bases.
* **Andrew M. Bruckner, *Differentiation of Real Functions***. A
  focused account of the pathologies: Dini derivatives, monotonicity,
  and typical behaviour.
* **Juha Heinonen, *Lectures on Lipschitz Analysis***. Lipschitz
  functions, extension, differentiability, and the route from real
  analysis into metric geometry.
* **Pertti Mattila, *Geometry of Sets and Measures in Euclidean
  Spaces***. Densities, rectifiability, and tangent measures,
  developed with real-analysis rather than current-theoretic tools.
* **Ralph Henstock, *The General Theory of Integration***. The
  originator's account of the gauge integral.
* **Russell A. Gordon, *The Integrals of Lebesgue, Denjoy, Perron, and
  Henstock***. A comparison of the nonabsolute integrals, which still
  appear in questions about differentiation and in the Fourier
  transform of unbounded functions.
* **A. M. Garsia, *Topics in Almost Everywhere Convergence***. A short
  classic on almost-everywhere convergence methods.

## 6. Probability: Measure-Theoretic Foundations and Limit Theory

Probability is now part of the core analytic landscape rather than a
neighbouring subject.

* **Patrick Billingsley, *Probability and Measure***. The classic
  first graduate text: measure-theoretic probability, characteristic
  functions, convergence, and central limit theory.
* **Rick Durrett, *Probability: Theory and Examples***. The most
  widely used modern graduate text, strong on martingales, Markov
  chains, Brownian motion, and limit theorems.
* **David Williams, *Probability with Martingales***. Elegant and
  concise; still the best short introduction to martingale language.
* **Olav Kallenberg, *Foundations of Modern Probability***. The
  comprehensive modern reference: conditioning, martingales, point
  processes, random measures, exchangeability, invariance, and
  stochastic calculus, all at high density.
* **Achim Klenke, *Probability Theory: A Comprehensive Course***. A
  broad modern graduate text with a clean measure-theoretic foundation
  and unusually wide coverage.
* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Probability Theory: An Analytic
  View***. Probability developed by an analyst, with semigroups, weak
  convergence, and analytic methods prominent throughout.
* **A. N. Shiryaev, *Probability***. Broad and detailed, bridging
  foundations, martingales, stochastic processes, and applications.
* **Leo Breiman, *Probability***. Older but still valuable, especially
  for technique and probabilistic intuition.
* **Yuan Shih Chow and Henry Teicher, *Probability Theory:
  Independence, Interchangeability, Martingales***. A classical
  graduate reference for limit theory and dependence structures.
* **R. M. Dudley, *Real Analysis and Probability***. A distinctive
  synthesis of measure theory, probability, weak convergence, and
  empirical processes.
* **Kai Lai Chung, *A Course in Probability Theory***. A careful
  classical treatment, especially good on characteristic functions and
  sums.
* **William Feller, *An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its
  Applications I–II***. Not measure-theoretic in volume I, but volume
  II remains one of the great expositions of limit theory, renewal
  theory, and Fourier methods in probability.
* **V. V. Petrov, *Sums of Independent Random Variables*** and
  ***Limit Theorems of Probability Theory***. The classical monographs
  behind precise asymptotic probability: rates, large deviations, and
  refinements of the central limit theorem.
* **A. A. Borovkov, *Probability Theory***. Broad, classical, and
  strong on asymptotic and limit-theorem content.
* **Patrick Billingsley, *Convergence of Probability Measures***. The
  standard source for weak convergence on metric spaces and functional
  limit theory.
* **K. R. Parthasarathy, *Probability Measures on Metric
  Spaces***. Foundational for tightness, Prokhorov's theorem, and
  probability on general state spaces.
* **B. V. Gnedenko and A. N. Kolmogorov, *Limit Distributions for Sums
  of Independent Random Variables***. The classical account of
  infinitely divisible and stable laws.
* **Ken-iti Sato, *Lévy Processes and Infinitely Divisible
  Distributions***. The definitive modern reference for Lévy processes
  and infinite divisibility.
* **Jean Bertoin, *Lévy Processes***. A concise research-level
  complement to Sato.
* **Michel Ledoux and Michel Talagrand, *Probability in Banach
  Spaces***. The major reference for random series, Gaussian
  processes, and concentration in infinite dimensions.
* **Robert J. Adler and Jonathan E. Taylor, *Random Fields and
  Geometry***. Gaussian fields, excursion sets, and the geometric side
  of continuous-parameter probability.
* **Amir Dembo and Ofer Zeitouni, *Large Deviations Techniques and
  Applications***. The standard reference for large deviation
  principles, Varadhan's lemma, and Sanov-type theorems.
* **Jean-Dominique Deuschel and Daniel W. Stroock, *Large
  Deviations***. An analyst's complement to Dembo–Zeitouni, closer to
  convexity and functional inequalities.
* **Frank den Hollander, *Large Deviations***. A short, readable entry
  to the same circle of ideas.

## 7. Concentration, High-Dimensional Probability, and Empirical Processes

A body of technique that now functions as analytic infrastructure well
outside probability proper.

* **Michel Ledoux, *The Concentration of Measure Phenomenon***. The
  standard monograph: isoperimetric, semigroup, and entropy routes to
  concentration.
* **Stéphane Boucheron, Gábor Lugosi, and Pascal Massart,
  *Concentration Inequalities: A Nonasymptotic Theory of
  Independence***. The most usable modern reference, organized around
  the entropy method and its variants.
* **Roman Vershynin, *High-Dimensional Probability: An Introduction
  with Applications in Data Science***. Sub-Gaussian technique, random
  matrices, and chaining, presented as a toolkit.
* **Michel Talagrand, *Upper and Lower Bounds for Stochastic
  Processes***. The definitive account of generic chaining and the
  majorizing measure theorem; difficult and essential.
* **Michel Talagrand, *The Generic Chaining***. The earlier and
  shorter version of the same programme.
* **Aad W. van der Vaart and Jon A. Wellner, *Weak Convergence and
  Empirical Processes***. The standard reference for empirical process
  theory, entropy conditions, and functional limit theorems.
* **David Pollard, *Convergence of Stochastic Processes*** and
  ***Empirical Processes: Theory and Applications***. Concise and
  influential, and often the clearest statement of a maximal
  inequality one needs.
* **Michel Ledoux and Michel Talagrand, *Probability in Banach
  Spaces***. Listed again: the geometric-probabilistic core from which
  much of this section grew.
* **Vitali D. Milman and Gideon Schechtman, *Asymptotic Theory of
  Finite Dimensional Normed Spaces***. Concentration on the sphere and
  the birth of asymptotic geometric analysis.
* **Shiri Artstein-Avidan, Apostolos Giannopoulos, and Vitali
  D. Milman, *Asymptotic Geometric Analysis I–II***. The modern
  systematic treatment of high-dimensional convexity, with strong
  analytic content.
* **Sergey G. Bobkov and Michel Ledoux, *One-dimensional Empirical
  Measures, Order Statistics, and Kantorovich Transport
  Distances***. A research monograph where concentration, transport,
  and empirical measures meet.
* **Greg W. Anderson, Alice Guionnet, and Ofer Zeitouni, *An
  Introduction to Random Matrices***. The standard graduate entry to
  random matrix theory, with free probability and large deviations
  included.
* **Terence Tao, *Topics in Random Matrix Theory***. A more discursive
  complement with an emphasis on technique.
* **Madan Lal Mehta, *Random Matrices***. The classical reference for
  exact computations and orthogonal-polynomial methods.
* **Percy Deift, *Orthogonal Polynomials and Random Matrices: A
  Riemann–Hilbert Approach***. The bridge to steepest-descent
  asymptotics, listed again in Part VIII.

## 8. Stochastic Processes, Martingales, and Stochastic Calculus

* **Ioannis Karatzas and Steven E. Shreve, *Brownian Motion and
  Stochastic Calculus***. The standard graduate course in
  continuous-time stochastic analysis and stochastic differential
  equations.
* **Daniel Revuz and Marc Yor, *Continuous Martingales and Brownian
  Motion***. The research-level standard: continuous martingales,
  local time, stochastic integration, excursions, and Bessel
  processes.
* **L. C. G. Rogers and David Williams, *Diffusions, Markov Processes,
  and Martingales I–II***. One of the great expositions, and unusually
  good at explaining why the general theory is organized as it is.
* **Peter Mörters and Yuval Peres, *Brownian Motion***. A modern,
  geometric, sample-path-oriented account with strong coverage of
  dimension and fractal properties.
* **Stewart N. Ethier and Thomas G. Kurtz, *Markov Processes:
  Characterization and Convergence***. The definitive reference for
  generators, martingale problems, and convergence of processes.
* **Daniel W. Stroock and S. R. Srinivasa Varadhan, *Multidimensional
  Diffusion Processes***. The foundational martingale-problem
  treatment of diffusions.
* **Nobuyuki Ikeda and Shinzo Watanabe, *Stochastic Differential
  Equations and Diffusion Processes***. A classical rigorous account
  of SDEs, diffusions, and stochastic flows, with the Japanese-school
  perspective on excursions and Malliavin calculus.
* **Bernt Øksendal, *Stochastic Differential Equations***. The
  standard accessible first course.
* **Philip Protter, *Stochastic Integration and Differential
  Equations***. The semimartingale approach, developed cleanly from
  the general theory.
* **Jean Jacod and Albert N. Shiryaev, *Limit Theorems for Stochastic
  Processes***. The major technical reference for semimartingale
  convergence and the asymptotics of processes.
* **Claude Dellacherie and Paul-André Meyer, *Probabilities and
  Potential*** (multi-volume). The monumental general theory of
  processes and the potential-theoretic foundations of stochastic
  analysis.
* **Sheng-Wu He, Jia-Gang Wang, and Jia-An Yan, *Semimartingale Theory
  and Stochastic Calculus***. A large and precise reference, useful
  where Dellacherie–Meyer is hard to navigate.
* **Hiroshi Kunita, *Stochastic Flows and Stochastic Differential
  Equations***. The standard monograph on stochastic flows of
  diffeomorphisms.
* **Richard F. Bass, *Stochastic Processes*** and ***Diffusions and
  Elliptic Operators***. Compact and analytically oriented, with the
  probabilistic–PDE dictionary made explicit.
* **Kai Lai Chung and Zhongxin Zhao, *From Brownian Motion to
  Schrödinger's Equation***. A bridge between Markov processes,
  potential theory, and spectral analysis.
* **Sidney C. Port and Charles J. Stone, *Brownian Motion and
  Classical Potential Theory***. The probabilistic route into
  classical potential theory.
* **Martin T. Barlow, *Random Walks and Heat Kernels on
  Graphs***. Discrete analogues of the whole continuous theory, and
  now a standard reference in its own right.
* **David A. Levin and Yuval Peres, *Markov Chains and Mixing
  Times***. The standard reference for quantitative convergence of
  Markov chains.
* **Sean Meyn and Richard L. Tweedie, *Markov Chains and Stochastic
  Stability***. General state-space Markov chains, ergodicity, and
  drift conditions.
* **N. V. Krylov, *Controlled Diffusion Processes***. PDE and
  probabilistic methods in diffusion control.
* **Kiyosi Itô and Henry P. McKean, Jr., *Diffusion Processes and
  Their Sample Paths***. A classic whose sample-path viewpoint has not
  been superseded.

## 9. Malliavin Calculus, Rough Paths, and Stochastic PDE

* **David Nualart, *The Malliavin Calculus and Related Topics***. The
  standard reference for the stochastic calculus of variations, Wiener
  chaos, and smoothness of densities.
* **Paul Malliavin, *Stochastic Analysis*** and ***Integration and
  Probability***. The originator's own accounts, geometric and
  demanding.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Differentiable Measures and the Malliavin
  Calculus***. The best single book for the measure-theoretic
  foundations, and the natural companion to Nualart.
* **Giuseppe Da Prato, *An Introduction to Infinite-Dimensional
  Analysis***. Gaussian measures, Sobolev spaces on Wiener space, and
  Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroups, compactly presented.
* **Terry Lyons and Zhongmin Qian, *System Control and Rough
  Paths***. The original systematic account of rough path theory.
* **Peter K. Friz and Nicolas B. Victoir, *Multidimensional Stochastic
  Processes as Rough Paths***. The comprehensive reference, with the
  Gaussian theory developed in detail.
* **Peter K. Friz and Martin Hairer, *A Course on Rough Paths***. The
  standard modern course, including an introduction to regularity
  structures.
* **Giuseppe Da Prato and Jerzy Zabczyk, *Stochastic Equations in
  Infinite Dimensions***. The basic analytic reference for SPDE via
  semigroups and infinite-dimensional stochastic integration.
* **Giuseppe Da Prato and Jerzy Zabczyk, *Ergodicity for Infinite
  Dimensional Systems***. Invariant measures and long-time behaviour
  of stochastic evolution equations.
* **Claudia Prévôt and Michael Röckner, *A Concise Course on
  Stochastic Partial Differential Equations***. The variational
  approach, short and usable.
* **S. Peszat and J. Zabczyk, *Stochastic Partial Differential
  Equations with Lévy Noise***. The standard reference beyond Gaussian
  forcing.
* **John B. Walsh, *An Introduction to Stochastic Partial Differential
  Equations***. The Saint-Flour notes that introduced the random-field
  approach.
* **Martin Hairer, *A Theory of Regularity Structures*** and the
  associated lecture notes. The research foundation for singular SPDE;
  still primarily a paper literature, but these notes are the entry
  point.
* **Lorenzo Zambotti, Massimiliano Gubinelli, and collaborators'
  lecture notes** on paracontrolled distributions offer the
  harmonic-analysis route to the same problems.

## 10. Ergodic Theory and Measurable Dynamics

* **Peter Walters, *An Introduction to Ergodic Theory***. The standard
  concise graduate introduction to measure-preserving dynamics,
  entropy, and topological dynamics.
* **Karl Petersen, *Ergodic Theory***. A classical systematic
  treatment of measure-preserving transformations.
* **Manfred Einsiedler and Thomas Ward, *Ergodic Theory with a View
  Towards Number Theory*** and the companion volume ***Homogeneous
  Dynamics and Applications***. The best modern route into homogeneous
  dynamics and its arithmetic uses.
* **I. P. Cornfeld, S. V. Fomin, and Ya. G. Sinai, *Ergodic
  Theory***. The large classical reference for entropy, K-systems, and
  measurable dynamics.
* **Ulrich Krengel, *Ergodic Theorems***. The definitive
  functional-analytic treatment of pointwise, mean, and subadditive
  ergodic theorems.
* **Tanja Eisner, Bálint Farkas, Markus Haase, and Rainer Nagel,
  *Operator Theoretic Aspects of Ergodic Theory***. The modern
  functional-analytic reformulation, and the natural sequel to
  Krengel.
* **Harry Furstenberg, *Recurrence in Ergodic Theory and Combinatorial
  Number Theory***. The founding text of the ergodic-theoretic
  approach to Szemerédi-type problems.
* **Bernard Host and Bryna Kra, *Nilpotent Structures in Ergodic
  Theory***. The definitive account of characteristic factors,
  nilsystems, and higher-order recurrence.
* **Luis Barreira and Yakov Pesin, *Introduction to Smooth Ergodic
  Theory***. Lyapunov exponents, entropy, and Pesin theory.
* **Anatole Katok and Boris Hasselblatt, *Introduction to the Modern
  Theory of Dynamical Systems***. The standard large graduate
  reference for smooth and hyperbolic dynamics.
* **Jon Aaronson, *An Introduction to Infinite Ergodic Theory***. The
  standard monograph for conservative transformations with infinite
  invariant measure.
* **Eli Glasner, *Ergodic Theory via Joinings***. A research monograph
  organized around joinings and structural theory.
* **M. G. Nadkarni, *Basic Ergodic Theory***. A compact first course
  emphasizing the spectral and measurable viewpoint.
* **David Ruelle, *Thermodynamic Formalism***. The foundational
  monograph on transfer operators, Gibbs states, and equilibrium
  measures.
* **Rufus Bowen, *Equilibrium States and the Ergodic Theory of Anosov
  Diffeomorphisms***. Short, foundational, and still the cleanest
  treatment of symbolic dynamics for hyperbolic systems.
* **William Parry and Mark Pollicott, *Zeta Functions and the Periodic
  Orbit Structure of Hyperbolic Dynamics***. The standard bridge from
  symbolic dynamics to zeta functions and orbit counting.
* **Viviane Baladi, *Positive Transfer Operators and Decay of
  Correlations*** and ***Dynamical Zeta Functions and Dynamical
  Determinants for Hyperbolic Maps***. The functional-analytic theory
  of transfer operators, including the anisotropic Banach space
  methods.
* **Robert J. Zimmer, *Ergodic Theory and Semisimple
  Groups***. Rigidity, cocycles, and group actions; the origin of a
  large part of modern measurable group theory.
* **Mark Pollicott and Michiko Yuri, *Dynamical Systems and Ergodic
  Theory***. A readable modern textbook bridging topological and
  measurable dynamics.

# Part III. Inequalities, Convexity, and Order

The literatures are genuinely different: the classical inequality
tradition, the convex-analytic tradition, the matrix tradition, and
the functional-inequality tradition have distinct canons and largely
disjoint citation patterns.

---

## 11. Classical Inequalities, Means, Majorization, and Rearrangement

* **G. H. Hardy, J. E. Littlewood, and G. Pólya, *Inequalities***. The
  founding monograph for means, convexity, majorization,
  rearrangements, and classical integral inequalities; still the book
  whose table of contents defines the subject.
* **E. F. Beckenbach and Richard Bellman, *Inequalities***. The
  complementary classical source, with differential, integral, and
  matrix inequalities and a distinctly mid-century flavour.
* **D. S. Mitrinović, *Analytic Inequalities***. The single most
  useful compendium for locating a named classical inequality and its
  variants.
* **D. S. Mitrinović, J. E. Pečarić, and A. M. Fink, *Classical and
  New Inequalities in Analysis*** and ***Inequalities Involving
  Functions and Their Integrals and Derivatives***. Large systematic
  compendia of refinements, converses, and generalizations.
* **A. W. Marshall, I. Olkin, and B. C. Arnold, *Inequalities: Theory
  of Majorization and Its Applications***. The standard modern
  reference for majorization, Schur convexity, doubly stochastic maps,
  and their remarkably wide applications.
* **P. S. Bullen, *Handbook of Means and Their Inequalities***. The
  systematic reference for classical and generalized means, power
  means, and the relations among them.
* **P. S. Bullen, D. S. Mitrinović, and P. M. Vasić, *Means and Their
  Inequalities***. The earlier and more theorem-oriented companion to
  the *Handbook*.
* **Elliott H. Lieb and Michael Loss, *Analysis***. Rearrangement
  inequalities, Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev, sharp constants, and a
  distinctively physical motivation throughout; the best modern book
  on sharp inequalities.
* **Almut Burchard's lecture notes on rearrangement inequalities** and
  **Bernd Kawohl, *Rearrangements and Convexity of Level Sets in
  PDE***. The two standard entry points to symmetrization as a PDE
  technique.
* **Yu. D. Burago and V. A. Zalgaller, *Geometric
  Inequalities***. Isoperimetry, mixed volumes, and comparison
  inequalities from the geometric side.
* **Rolf Schneider, *Convex Bodies: The Brunn–Minkowski Theory***. The
  definitive reference for the Brunn–Minkowski inequality and its many
  descendants.
* **Erwin Lutwak, Richard Gardner's *Geometric Tomography*, and
  Gardner's survey on the Brunn–Minkowski inequality**. The natural
  continuations for anyone whose inequalities are geometric.
* **G. Pólya and G. Szegő, *Problems and Theorems in Analysis
  I–II***. An inexhaustible source of classical inequalities together
  with the techniques that prove them.
* **G. Pólya and G. Szegő, *Isoperimetric Inequalities in Mathematical
  Physics***. The classical source for the shape-optimization
  tradition in eigenvalue and capacity inequalities.
* **Nicholas D. Kazarinoff, *Analytic Inequalities***. A short and
  pedagogically excellent introduction.
* **Constantin P. Niculescu and Lars-Erik Persson, *Convex Functions
  and Their Applications: A Contemporary Approach***. Modern,
  well-organized, and good on Jensen-type inequalities in their many
  forms.
* **Josip Pečarić, Frank Proschan, and Y. L. Tong, *Convex Functions,
  Partial Orderings, and Statistical Applications***. Majorization and
  convex ordering in a form useful well beyond statistics.
* **Vladimir Maz'ya, *Sobolev Spaces***. Listed again in Part V, but
  indispensable here for sharp constants, capacitary inequalities, and
  necessary-and-sufficient conditions.
* **Dorin Bucur and Giuseppe Buttazzo, *Variational Methods in Shape
  Optimization Problems***. Where inequalities for eigenvalues and
  capacities become a variational subject.

## 12. Convexity, Convex Analysis, and Variational Analysis

* **R. Tyrrell Rockafellar, *Convex Analysis***. The foundational
  modern monograph: convex sets, conjugacy, subgradients, and duality
  in finite dimensions.
* **R. Tyrrell Rockafellar and Roger J.-B. Wets, *Variational
  Analysis***. The research-level extension to nonsmooth, set-valued,
  and epigraphical analysis; dense but definitive.
* **Jean-Baptiste Hiriart-Urruty and Claude Lemaréchal, *Convex
  Analysis and Minimization Algorithms I–II*** and the abridged
  ***Fundamentals of Convex Analysis***. The most readable systematic
  treatment of finite-dimensional convexity.
* **Jonathan M. Borwein and Adrian S. Lewis, *Convex Analysis and
  Nonlinear Optimization***. Convex duality, cones, entropy, and
  modern applications, with unusually good exercises.
* **Jonathan M. Borwein and Jon D. Vanderwerff, *Convex Functions:
  Constructions, Characterizations and Counterexamples***. The
  infinite-dimensional theory and its pathologies.
* **Heinz H. Bauschke and Patrick L. Combettes, *Convex Analysis and
  Monotone Operator Theory in Hilbert Spaces***. The modern
  Hilbert-space synthesis of convexity, monotone operators,
  projections, and proximal methods.
* **Ivar Ekeland and Roger Temam, *Convex Analysis and Variational
  Problems***. The classic bridge from convex duality to PDE and the
  calculus of variations.
* **Viorel Barbu and Teodor Precupanu, *Convexity and Optimization in
  Banach Spaces***. The Banach-space theory, carefully done.
* **Frank H. Clarke, *Optimization and Nonsmooth Analysis***. The
  foundational monograph on generalized gradients and nonsmooth
  calculus.
* **Boris S. Mordukhovich, *Variational Analysis and Generalized
  Differentiation I–II***. The comprehensive modern treatment of
  limiting subdifferentials and their calculus.
* **Aleksandr D. Alexandrov's convexity work, and Herbert Busemann,
  *Convex Surfaces***. The classical geometric strand, still relevant
  to second-order differentiability results.
* **Roger Webster, *Convexity***. A clean elementary account of
  finite-dimensional convexity.
* **Steven R. Lay, *Convex Sets and Their Applications***. Convex sets
  at the same elementary level, with geometric applications.
* **A. Ben-Tal and A. Nemirovski, *Lectures on Modern Convex
  Optimization***. Included because conic duality and the structure of
  convex cones have become common analytic vocabulary.
* **Barry Simon, *Convexity: An Analytic Viewpoint***. Choquet theory,
  Krein–Milman, Loewner and Bernstein theorems, and convexity as it is
  actually used by analysts.
* **Robert R. Phelps, *Lectures on Choquet's Theorem*** and ***Convex
  Functions, Monotone Operators and Differentiability***. The two
  standard short monographs on integral representation and on
  differentiability of convex functions in Banach spaces.
* **Gustave Choquet, *Lectures on Analysis I–III***. Idiosyncratic and
  wide-ranging, with the original treatment of integral representation
  and capacities.

## 13. Matrix Analysis, Operator Monotonicity, and Noncommutative Inequalities

* **Rajendra Bhatia, *Matrix Analysis***. The standard graduate
  monograph on matrix inequalities, majorization, singular values,
  unitarily invariant norms, and perturbation of eigenvalues.
* **Rajendra Bhatia, *Positive Definite Matrices***. Matrix means, the
  geometry of the positive cone, operator convexity, and the Loewner
  order.
* **Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*** and
  ***Topics in Matrix Analysis***. Large references for matrix
  inequalities, spectral comparison, norms, canonical forms, and
  Hadamard and Kronecker products.
* **Fumio Hiai and Dénes Petz, *Introduction to Matrix Analysis and
  Applications***. Matrix monotonicity, convexity, entropy, and the
  noncommutative inequalities used in quantum information.
* **Fumio Hiai and Dénes Petz, *Quantum Entropy and Its
  Use***. Relative entropy, monotonicity under completely positive
  maps, and the operator-inequality background to quantum statistical
  mechanics.
* **Karl Löwner's original work, as presented in Bhatia and in
  Donoghue**: **William F. Donoghue, Jr., *Monotone Matrix Functions
  and Analytic Continuation***. The classical monograph linking
  operator monotonicity to Pick functions and Nevanlinna
  representation.
* **Barry Simon, *Loewner's Theorem on Monotone Matrix Functions***. A
  modern book-length treatment with many proofs of the central theorem
  and its surroundings.
* **Barry Simon, *Trace Ideals and Their Applications***. Schatten
  classes, determinants, and trace inequalities; the standard
  reference for the infinite-dimensional case.
* **I. C. Gohberg and M. G. Krein, *Introduction to the Theory of
  Linear Nonselfadjoint Operators***. Singular numbers, s-number
  inequalities, and the classical theory of trace-class perturbation.
* **Albrecht Pietsch, *Eigenvalues and s-Numbers***. The systematic
  theory of approximation numbers and eigenvalue estimates for
  operator ideals.
* **Peter Lancaster and Miron Tismenetsky, *The Theory of
  Matrices***. A large general reference for matrix theory.
* **Alexander Markus, *Introduction to the Spectral Theory of
  Polynomial Operator Pencils***. Spectral theory of polynomial
  pencils, for when a matrix question is really spectral.
* **Gerd Grubb and Joachim Weidmann** for the differential-operator
  analogues, and **Michael Sh. Birman and Michael Solomyak** for
  eigenvalue asymptotics; see Part IV.
* **Eric Carlen, *Trace Inequalities and Quantum Entropy***. A set of
  lecture notes that has become the standard modern introduction.

## 14. Functional, Isoperimetric, and Geometric Inequalities

* **Vladimir Maz'ya, *Sobolev Spaces, with Applications to Elliptic
  Partial Differential Equations***. The encyclopedic source for
  Sobolev, capacitary, and isoperimetric-type inequalities, with
  necessary and sufficient conditions rather than merely sufficient
  ones.
* **Dominique Bakry, Ivan Gentil, and Michel Ledoux, *Analysis and
  Geometry of Markov Diffusion Operators***. Γ-calculus,
  curvature–dimension conditions, logarithmic Sobolev, Poincaré, and
  Nash inequalities in a single framework; the central modern
  reference.
* **Laurent Saloff-Coste, *Aspects of Sobolev-Type
  Inequalities***. Sobolev, Nash, Poincaré, and heat-kernel
  inequalities on manifolds and graphs.
* **Alexander Grigor'yan, *Heat Kernel and Analysis on
  Manifolds***. Functional inequalities as they control heat kernels
  and volume growth.
* **Thierry Aubin, *Nonlinear Analysis on Manifolds: Monge–Ampère
  Equations*** and ***Some Nonlinear Problems in Riemannian
  Geometry***. Sharp Sobolev constants, best-constant problems, and
  the Yamabe circle of ideas.
* **Emmanuel Hebey, *Nonlinear Analysis on Manifolds: Sobolev Spaces
  and Inequalities***. The functional-analytic complement to Aubin,
  careful about sharp constants and concentration.
* **Cédric Villani, *Optimal Transport: Old and New***. Among other
  things, the most complete account of transport proofs of functional
  inequalities.
* **Michel Ledoux, *The Concentration of Measure Phenomenon***. Listed
  again: isoperimetry and concentration are two views of one subject.
* **Christer Borell's work on Gaussian isoperimetry, and Bogachev's
  *Gaussian Measures***. The Gaussian case, where the sharp results
  are known.
* **Antoine Henrot, *Extremum Problems for Eigenvalues of Elliptic
  Operators***. Faber–Krahn, Szegő–Weinberger, and the modern
  shape-optimization treatment of spectral inequalities.
* **Isaac Chavel, *Isoperimetric Inequalities: Differential Geometric
  and Analytic Perspectives***. The geometric side, written for
  analysts.
* **Giovanni Alberti, Luigi Ambrosio and others on quantitative
  isoperimetry**, best approached through **Nicola Fusco's surveys**
  and **Francesco Maggi, *Sets of Finite Perimeter and Geometric
  Variational Problems***.

## 15. Core Banach and Hilbert Space Theory

* **John B. Conway, *A Course in Functional Analysis***. The standard

* **Peter D. Lax, *Functional Analysis***. A distinctive analyst's

* **Gert K. Pedersen, *Analysis Now***. Compact and high-level, moving

* **Kôsaku Yosida, *Functional Analysis***. A classical comprehensive

* **Barbara MacCluer, *Elementary Functional Analysis***. Short and

* **Robert E. Megginson, *An Introduction to Banach Space Theory***. A

* **Robert E. Edwards, *Functional Analysis: Theory and

* **F. Riesz and B. Sz.-Nagy, *Functional Analysis***. A historical

* **Marián Fabian, Petr Habala, Petr Hájek, Vicente Montesinos, and

## 16. Locally Convex Spaces, Duality, and Distributions

* **François Trèves, *Topological Vector Spaces, Distributions and

* **Hans Jarchow, *Locally Convex Spaces***. A systematic modern

* **Reinhold Meise and Dietmar Vogt, *Introduction to Functional

* **John Horváth, *Topological Vector Spaces and Distributions***. A

* **Alexander Grothendieck, *Topological Vector Spaces*** and the

* **Raymond A. Ryan, *Introduction to Tensor Products of Banach

* **Andreas Defant and Klaus Floret, *Tensor Norms and Operator

* **Nicolas Bourbaki, *Topological Vector Spaces***. The structural

* **Pedro Pérez Carreras and José Bonet, *Barrelled Locally Convex

* **Laurent Schwartz, *Théorie des distributions***. The original

* **I. M. Gel'fand and G. E. Shilov, *Generalized Functions***

* **Robert S. Strichartz, *A Guide to Distribution Theory and Fourier
  Transforms***. A concise modern first route for analysts.
* **F. G. Friedlander and Mark Joshi, *Introduction to the Theory of
  Distributions***. Short, clean, and PDE-facing.
* **V. S. Vladimirov, *Methods of the Theory of Generalized

* **J. F. Colombeau, *New Generalized Functions and Multiplication of

* **Serge Lang, *Real and Functional Analysis***. Included here for

* **Joseph Diestel and J. J. Uhl, Jr., *Vector Measures***. The

## 17. Banach Space Geometry, Bases, and Operator Ideals

* **Fernando Albiac and Nigel J. Kalton, *Topics in Banach Space

* **Nicole Tomczak-Jaegermann, *Banach–Mazur Distances and

* **Albrecht Pietsch, *Operator Ideals***. The classical systematic

* **Joseph Diestel and J. J. Uhl, Jr., *Vector Measures***. The

* **Bernard Beauzamy, *Introduction to Banach Spaces and Their

## 18. Vector-Valued Analysis and Analysis in Banach Spaces

A body of technique that did not exist as a unified subject when most
of the older references were written, and that now underpins maximal
regularity, stochastic analysis in Banach spaces, and much of modern
harmonic analysis.

* **Tuomas Hytönen, Jan van Neerven, Mark Veraar, and Lutz Weis,
  *Analysis in Banach Spaces I–III*** (*Martingales and
  Littlewood–Paley Theory*; *Probabilistic Methods and Operator
  Theory*; *Harmonic Analysis and Spectral Theory*). The definitive
  modern treatise: UMD spaces, R-boundedness, vector-valued singular
  integrals, H-infinity calculus, and maximal regularity.
* **Joseph Diestel and J. J. Uhl, Jr., *Vector Measures***. The
  measure-theoretic foundation.
* **Jan van Neerven, *Stochastic Evolution Equations*** lecture notes
  and **the γ-radonifying operator literature**. The bridge from this
  section to Part II.
* **Herbert Amann, *Linear and Quasilinear Parabolic
  Problems***. Where vector-valued function spaces become an
  indispensable PDE tool.
* **Alessandra Lunardi, *Interpolation Theory*** and ***Analytic
  Semigroups and Optimal Regularity in Parabolic Problems***. Real
  interpolation for vector-valued spaces, done carefully.
* **Wolfgang Arendt, Charles J. K. Batty, Matthias Hieber, and Frank
  Neubrander, *Vector-Valued Laplace Transforms and Cauchy
  Problems***. The Laplace-transform theory in the vector-valued
  setting, with asymptotics.
* **Markus Haase, *The Functional Calculus for Sectorial
  Operators***. The standard reference for holomorphic functional
  calculus, and the natural companion to Hytönen et al.
* **Peer Christian Kunstmann and Lutz Weis's lecture notes on maximal
  L^p-regularity**. The standard survey that organized the field
  before the treatise above appeared.

* **W. A. J. Luxemburg and A. C. Zaanen, *Riesz Spaces I*** and

## 20. Banach Algebras and Commutative Harmonic Analysis of Algebras

* **Frank F. Bonsall and John Duncan, *Complete Normed
  Algebras***. The standard structural reference for Banach algebras,
  numerical range, and representation theory.
* **Charles E. Rickart, *General Theory of Banach Algebras***. The
  classical comprehensive treatment.
* **Theodore W. Gamelin, *Uniform Algebras***. The definitive book on

* **Robert E. Greene and Steven G. Krantz, *Function Theory of One

## 41. Classical Function Theory: Entire and Meromorphic Functions, Value Distribution

# Suggested Reading Paths

No analyst reads a bibliography linearly. The more useful way to
interpret "core knowledge" is by layers.

## A. First graduate year: a common foundation

1. **Real analysis and measure**: Folland, Rudin, Royden–Fitzpatrick,
   or Stein–Shakarchi *Real Analysis*.
2. **Functional analysis**: Conway, Rudin, Brezis, or Stein–Shakarchi
   *Functional Analysis*.
3. **Complex analysis**: Ahlfors, Conway, Stein–Shakarchi, Gamelin, or
   Remmert.
4. **PDE**: Evans, Taylor I, Folland, or Renardy–Rogers.
5. **Probability, where it is part of the programme**: Billingsley,
   Durrett, Williams, or Kallenberg used selectively.

By the end of this stage one should be fluent in distributions, weak
convergence, Sobolev spaces, the Fourier transform, and basic spectral
theory, whether or not any course was labelled with those names.

## B. Second-course infrastructure

The books that most change how a graduate student actually proves
things:

- Stein, *Singular Integrals*, and Grafakos I;
- Adams–Fournier or Leoni on Sobolev spaces;
- Bergh–Löfström on interpolation;
- Hörmander I on distributions and Fourier analysis;
- Kato on operators, and Reed–Simon I–II;
- Engel–Nagel or Pazy on semigroups;
- Gilbarg–Trudinger on elliptic PDE;
- Evans–Gariepy on fine properties;
- Rockafellar on convex analysis, and Bauschke–Combettes on monotone
  operators;
- Kallenberg or Durrett for probability, with Ledoux–Talagrand or
  Boucheron–Lugosi–Massart for concentration;
- Villani, *Topics in Optimal Transportation*, which has become a
  common second language.

## C. Research entry by direction

**Harmonic analysis.** Stein's two monographs and Grafakos I–II, then
Muscalu–Schlag, Lerner–Nazarov and Cruz-Uribe–Martell–Pérez for the
sparse and weighted theory, Demeter for decoupling, and Wolff or
Mattila for the geometric side.

**Linear and nonlinear PDE.** Hörmander I–IV; Gilbarg–Trudinger,
Ladyzhenskaya–Ural'tseva, and Krylov for elliptic and parabolic
regularity; Caffarelli–Cabré and Crandall–Ishii–Lions for the fully
nonlinear theory; Dafermos and Bressan for conservation laws; Tao,
Cazenave, and Bahouri–Chemin–Danchin for dispersive equations.

**Functional and operator analysis.** Kato, Reed–Simon,
Dunford–Schwartz; Sz.-Nagy–Foiaş and Nikolski for model theory;
Takesaki and Kadison–Ringrose for operator algebras;
Lindenstrauss–Tzafriri, Pisier, and Albiac–Kalton for Banach spaces;
Hytönen–van Neerven–Veraar–Weis for the vector-valued theory.

**Geometric measure theory and variational analysis.** Maggi and Simon
first, then Federer as a reference; Ambrosio–Fusco–Pallara for BV;
Dacorogna and Dal Maso for variational convergence;
Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré and Villani for transport and gradient flows;
David–Semmes and Tolsa for quantitative rectifiability.

**Complex analysis, one variable.** Burckel or Remmert for the
classical theory in full; Garnett and Duren for Hardy spaces;
Pommerenke and Garnett–Marshall for boundary behaviour and harmonic
measure; Levin and Koosis for entire functions and the logarithmic
integral; Nikolski for the operator-theoretic direction.

**Complex analysis, several variables.** Hörmander and Range first;
then Chen–Shaw and Straube for the d-bar-Neumann problem; Demailly and
Forstnerič for the geometric and Oka-theoretic directions; Klimek and
Guedj–Zeriahi for pluripotential theory; Baouendi–Ebenfelt–Rothschild
for CR mappings.

**Probability and stochastic analysis.** Kallenberg, Revuz–Yor,
Rogers–Williams, Ethier–Kurtz, Stroock–Varadhan; then Nualart for
Malliavin calculus, Friz–Hairer for rough paths, and Da Prato–Zabczyk
for SPDE.

**Special functions and asymptotics.** Andrews–Askey–Roy and the DLMF;
Olver and Wong for asymptotics; Ismail and Simon for orthogonal
polynomials; Costin or Balser for summability; Deift for the
Riemann–Hilbert method.

**Spectral theory and mathematical physics.** Reed–Simon and Kato;
Teschl or Schmüdgen for a modern foundation; Zworski and
Dimassi–Sjöstrand for the semiclassical theory; Dyatlov–Zworski for
resonances.

## D. A note on how to use a list like this

Most of these books will never be read completely by anyone, and that
is not a defect. The practical skill is knowing which book a given
question belongs to. It is worth spending an hour with the table of
contents of a major reference before needing it, so that later the
memory to be retrieved is "this is a Maz'ya question" or "this is a
Burckel question" rather than a search.

# What This Bibliography Deliberately Does Not Try to Do

- **Neighbouring fields appear only where they support analytic
  practice.** Algebraic geometry, differential topology,
  representation theory, and logic are represented by the specific
  books analysts actually open.
- **Numerical analysis and scientific computing are largely omitted**,
  despite their deep interaction with approximation theory, PDE, and
  spectral theory. A few exceptions are admitted where the book is the
  best available account of a piece of analysis (Trefethen, de Boor,
  LeVeque, Peyré–Cuturi).
- **Mathematical statistics is omitted** except where it coincides
  with probability, empirical processes, or asymptotics.
- **Analytic number theory is omitted as a subject**, although many of
  its tools are harmonic or complex analytic; Montgomery's *Ten
  Lectures* is included precisely because it sits on the
  boundary. Readers wanting the subject itself should begin with
  Iwaniec–Kowalski.
- **Mathematical physics is included only where the analysis is the
  point** — spectral theory, scattering, statistical mechanics of
  operator algebras, kinetic theory — and not otherwise.
- **Specialized literatures in stochastic PDE, random matrices,
  inverse problems, integrable systems, and free probability are
  represented by their nearest stable monographs** rather than by
  article lists, even where the research front is entirely in papers.
- **Research areas whose canonical literature is still primarily
  periodical** are represented by lecture notes and surveys where
  these have stabilized, and are otherwise omitted rather than guessed
  at.
- **No claim of completeness or of ranking is made.** Several
  excellent books are absent because they never became common
  reference points, and a few unfashionable books are present because
  they still are.

The operative test throughout: a book belongs here if knowing that it
exists, and roughly what role it plays, is part of the working culture
of graduate or research-level analysis.
