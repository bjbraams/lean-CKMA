# Synopsis: Real analysis, measure, and integration

Part of the [mathematical synopsis series](SYNOPSIS.md).

This synopsis concerns the common graduate core of Section 04 of
[CKMA-bib.md](CKMA-bib.md) and [CKMA-bib2.md](CKMA-bib2.md): construction of
measures, Lebesgue integration, convergence, product measures, differentiation,
and the function spaces and representation theorems built from them. It also
includes the recurring structural results on Radon, invariant, and vector
integration. The selection follows overlapping treatments in the references;
specialized theories of Gaussian measures and measure algebras require separate
development.

Throughout, $(X,\mathcal A,\mu)$ is a measure space; functions are real or complex
unless indicated otherwise, and equality in $L^p$ means equality almost
everywhere. Measures are positive unless explicitly called signed or complex.
Euclidean measure is denoted by $m$ or $\mathcal L^n$.

A **null set** is a measurable set of measure zero; a property holds almost
everywhere if it fails only on a null set. A measure is **locally finite** on
a topological space if every point has a neighborhood of finite measure.
The **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** is generated by the open sets. It is distinct
from its completion: for example, Lebesgue measurable subsets of Euclidean
space need not be Borel, although each differs from a Borel set by a null set.
Countable additivity implies continuity from below for increasing sets, and
continuity from above for decreasing sets when the first set has finite measure.

## Measures and their construction

A **$\sigma$-algebra** is a family of subsets closed under complementation and
countable unions. A **measure** is a countably additive map
$\mu:\mathcal A\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu(\varnothing)=0$. It is **complete** if
every subset of a null set is measurable, and **$\sigma$-finite** if $X$ is a
countable union of sets of finite measure. An **outer measure** is defined on all
subsets and is monotone and countably subadditive, with value zero at the empty set.

* **1. Carathéodory's construction.** For an outer measure $\mu^*$, the sets $E$
  satisfying
  $$\mu^*(A)=\mu^*(A\cap E)+\mu^*(A\setminus E)\qquad(A\subseteq X)$$
  form a $\sigma$-algebra, and the restriction of $\mu^*$ to it is a complete measure.
* **2. Extension of a premeasure.** A countably additive premeasure on an algebra
  extends to the generated $\sigma$-algebra by the outer-measure construction.
  The extension is unique if the premeasure is $\sigma$-finite.
* **3. Uniqueness on generating classes.** Two finite measures agreeing on a
  generating $\pi$-system containing $X$ agree everywhere. The corresponding
  $\sigma$-finite statement holds if the generating class contains an increasing
  exhaustion on which both measures are finite. The underlying $\pi$–$\lambda$
  theorem identifies the Dynkin system generated by a $\pi$-system with its
  generated $\sigma$-algebra.
* **4. Completion.** Every measure has a canonical completion: its measurable
  sets are the sets differing from an original measurable set by a subset of an
  original null set. The extended measure is uniquely determined on this completion.
* **5. Lebesgue measure.** There is a unique translation-invariant Borel measure
  on $\mathbb R^n$ assigning the unit cube mass one. Its completion is Lebesgue
  measure; it is regular and satisfies $m(TA)=|\det T|m(A)$ for invertible linear
  maps $T$ and measurable $A$.
* **6. Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures.** An increasing right-continuous function
  $F:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ determines a unique locally finite Borel measure by
  $\mu_F((a,b])=F(b)-F(a)$. Conversely, every locally finite Borel measure on
  $\mathbb R$ has such a distribution function, unique up to an additive constant.

## The integral and its convergence theorems

A function is **measurable** when inverse images of Borel sets are measurable.
For $f\geq0$, the **Lebesgue integral** is the supremum of the integrals of
nonnegative simple functions below $f$. A scalar function is **integrable** when
$\int|f|\,d\mu<\infty$; its integral is obtained by positive and negative parts,
or real and imaginary parts.

* **7. Simple approximation.** Every nonnegative measurable function is the
  pointwise increasing limit of nonnegative measurable simple functions. Every
  integrable function can be approximated in $L^1$ by integrable simple functions.
* **8. Monotone convergence.** If $0\leq f_n\uparrow f$ almost everywhere, then
  $\int f_n\,d\mu\uparrow\int f\,d\mu$, with infinity allowed.
* **9. Fatou's lemma.** For nonnegative measurable $f_n$,
  $$\int\liminf_n f_n\,d\mu\leq\liminf_n\int f_n\,d\mu.$$
* **10. Dominated convergence.** If $f_n\to f$ almost everywhere and
  $|f_n|\leq g\in L^1$, then $f\in L^1$ and $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$; in particular,
  the integrals converge.
* **11. Integration of series.** For nonnegative measurable $f_n$, integration
  commutes with summation. If $\sum_n\|f_n\|_1<\infty$, then $\sum_n f_n$
  converges absolutely almost everywhere and in $L^1$, and its integral is the
  sum of the integrals.
* **12. Absolute continuity of the integral.** For $f\in L^1$ and $\varepsilon>0$
  there is $\delta>0$ such that $\mu(E)<\delta$ implies
  $\int_E|f|\,d\mu<\varepsilon$. Also $\int_{\{|f|>R\}}|f|\,d\mu\to0$.
* **13. Distribution and layer-cake formulas.** For measurable $f$ and $p>0$,
  $$\int |f|^p\,d\mu
    =p\int_0^\infty t^{p-1}\mu\{|f|>t\}\,dt.$$
  Both sides may be infinite.
* **14. Jensen's inequality.** On a probability space, for an integrable
  real-valued $f$ taking values in an interval and a finite convex function
  $\Phi$ on that interval,
  $\Phi(\int f\,d\mu)\leq\int\Phi(f)\,d\mu$ whenever the latter integral is
  defined. The usual integrability hypotheses give the corresponding conditional
  inequality.
* **15. Riemann and Lebesgue integrals.** A bounded real function on a compact
  interval is Riemann integrable exactly when its discontinuity set is Lebesgue
  null. In that case the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals agree.

## Modes of convergence and approximation

**Convergence in measure** means
$\mu\{|f_n-f|>\varepsilon\}\to0$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.
On a finite measure space a family $\mathcal F\subseteq L^1$ is **uniformly
integrable** if
$\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\int_{\{|f|>R\}}|f|\,d\mu\to0$ as $R\to\infty$.
On such a space, uniform integrability is equivalently a uniform $L^1$ bound
together with uniform absolute continuity of the integrals over sets of small
measure. For infinite measure spaces, global $L^1$ convergence additionally
requires control of mass escaping through the underlying space; local
convergence in measure by itself cannot supply this.

* **16. Markov–Chebyshev bounds.** For $p>0$ and $t>0$,
  $\mu\{|f|>t\}\leq t^{-p}\|f\|_p^p$. Thus $L^p$ convergence for finite $p$
  implies convergence in measure.
* **17. Subsequences and almost-everywhere convergence.** Convergence in measure
  has an almost-everywhere convergent subsequence. On a finite measure space,
  almost-everywhere convergence implies convergence in measure, and convergence
  in measure is equivalent to every subsequence having a further subsequence
  converging almost everywhere to the proposed limit.
* **18. Completeness for convergence in measure.** On a finite measure space,
  measurable finite-valued functions modulo null sets form a complete metric
  space for
  $$d(f,g)=\int\min(1,|f-g|)\,d\mu.$$
  This metric induces convergence in measure.
* **19. Egorov's theorem.** On a finite measure space, almost-everywhere
  convergence is uniform outside a measurable set of arbitrarily small measure.
* **20. Lusin's theorem.** If $\mu$ is a finite Radon measure on a locally compact
  Hausdorff space and $f$ is finite-valued and measurable for its completion,
  then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a compact $K$ with
  $\mu(X\setminus K)<\varepsilon$ such that $f|_K$ is continuous.
* **21. Vitali's convergence theorem.** On a finite measure space, if
  $f_n\to f$ in measure, then $f_n\to f$ in $L^1$ exactly when $\{f_n\}$ is
  uniformly integrable. In particular, the latter hypotheses imply $f\in L^1$.
* **22. Scheffé's lemma.** If $f_n,f\geq0$ are integrable, $f_n\to f$ almost
  everywhere, and $\int f_n\to\int f$, then $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$.

## Products, image measures, and parameter integrals

The **product $\sigma$-algebra** is generated by measurable rectangles. The
**image measure** of $\mu$ under a measurable $T:X\to Y$ is
$T_\#\mu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}B)$. A **kernel** $K(x,dy)$ assigns a measure to each $x$
and makes $x\mapsto K(x,B)$ measurable for each measurable $B$.

* **23. Product measure.** For $\sigma$-finite measure spaces there is a unique
  measure $\mu\otimes\nu$ on the product $\sigma$-algebra with
  $(\mu\otimes\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)$, with $0\cdot\infty=0$.
* **24. Tonelli's theorem.** For nonnegative product-measurable $f$ on a product
  of $\sigma$-finite spaces, section integrals are measurable and
  $$\int f\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)
    =\int\!\left(\int f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
    =\int\!\left(\int f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).$$
* **25. Fubini's theorem.** If $f\in L^1(\mu\otimes\nu)$, its sections are
  integrable almost everywhere, the section integrals are integrable, and both
  iterated integrals equal its product integral. The completed-product version
  has the corresponding almost-everywhere interpretation of sections.
* **26. Integration against an image measure.** For nonnegative measurable $g$,
  or for integrable $g$,
  $\int_Yg\,d(T_\#\mu)=\int_Xg\circ T\,d\mu$.
* **27. Integration with kernels.** For a probability kernel $K$ and a measure
  $\mu$, there is a measure $\mu(dx)K(x,dy)$ on the product characterized by
  iterated integration of nonnegative measurable functions. This includes
  composition of kernels and the usual joint-law construction.
* **28. Differentiation under the integral.** Suppose $t\mapsto f(t,x)$ is
  differentiable on an open interval for almost every $x$, $f(t_0,\cdot)\in L^1$
  for some $t_0$, and on each compact subinterval $|\partial_t f(t,x)|$ has an
  integrable majorant. Then
  $$\frac{d}{dt}\int f(t,x)\,d\mu(x)=\int\partial_t f(t,x)\,d\mu(x).$$

## Signed measures, variation, and densities

For finite measures, **absolute continuity** $\nu\ll\mu$ means that every
$\mu$-null set is $\nu$-null; **mutual singularity** $\nu\perp\mu$ means that
the measures are carried by disjoint measurable sets. The **total variation** of
a finite signed or complex measure is
$|\nu|(E)=\sup\sum_j|\nu(E_j)|$, over finite measurable partitions of $E$.

* **29. Hahn decomposition.** A finite signed measure admits a partition
  $X=P\sqcup N$ on every measurable subset of which it is respectively
  nonnegative and nonpositive. Such partitions agree modulo variation-null sets.
* **30. Jordan decomposition.** Every finite signed measure has a unique
  decomposition $\nu=\nu^+-\nu^-$ into mutually singular positive measures.
  Its variation is $|\nu|=\nu^++\nu^-$.
* **31. Radon–Nikodým theorem.** If $\mu,\nu$ are $\sigma$-finite positive
  measures and $\nu\ll\mu$, there is a nonnegative measurable density $h$, unique
  $\mu$-almost everywhere, with $\nu(E)=\int_Eh\,d\mu$. Finite signed and complex
  measures absolutely continuous with respect to $\sigma$-finite $\mu$ have
  densities in $L^1(\mu)$.
* **32. Lebesgue decomposition.** Relative to a $\sigma$-finite positive $\mu$,
  each finite positive, signed, or complex measure $\nu$ decomposes uniquely as
  $\nu=h\mu+\nu_s$, where $h\in L^1(\mu)$ and $\nu_s\perp\mu$.
* **33. Polar decomposition.** For a finite complex measure $\nu$ there is a
  measurable $u$ with $|u|=1$ almost everywhere for $|\nu|$ such that
  $d\nu=u\,d|\nu|$. Consequently
  $|\int f\,d\nu|\leq\int|f|\,d|\nu|$.
* **34. Calculus of densities.** For $\sigma$-finite positive measures
  $\lambda\ll\nu\ll\mu$,
  $$\frac{d\lambda}{d\mu}
    =\frac{d\lambda}{d\nu}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\quad\mu\text{-almost everywhere},$$
  with compatible versions on zero-density sets. For a measurable $h$ with
  $\int|h|\,d\mu<\infty$, $|h\mu|=|h|\mu$.

## $L^p$ spaces and the analytic operations on them

For $1\leq p<\infty$, $L^p(\mu)$ consists of measurable functions modulo null
sets with norm $\|f\|_p=(\int|f|^p\,d\mu)^{1/p}$; $L^\infty$ has the essential
supremum norm. Exponents $p,q$ are **conjugate** if $1/p+1/q=1$.

* **35. Hölder's inequality.** For conjugate $p,q$,
  $\|fg\|_1\leq\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$. In particular $L^2$ satisfies the
  Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
* **36. Minkowski's inequalities.** For $p\geq1$,
  $\|f+g\|_p\leq\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p$. For measurable $F$ on a product of
  $\sigma$-finite spaces,
  $$\left\|\int F(\cdot,y)\,d\nu(y)\right\|_p
    \leq\int\|F(\cdot,y)\|_p\,d\nu(y)$$
  whenever the right side is finite.
* **37. Riesz–Fischer completeness.** Every $L^p$, $1\leq p\leq\infty$, is a
  Banach space; $L^2$ is a Hilbert space with its integral inner product.
* **38. Density.** Integrable simple functions are dense in $L^p$ for
  $p<\infty$. For Radon measures on locally compact Hausdorff spaces, $C_c$ is
  dense in $L^p$, $p<\infty$; for Lebesgue measure on an open Euclidean set,
  $C_c^\infty$ is dense there as well.
* **39. Duality.** On a $\sigma$-finite space and for $1\leq p<\infty$,
  every continuous linear functional on $L^p$ has the form
  $f\mapsto\int f\overline g\,d\mu$ for a unique $g\in L^q$, and its norm is
  $\|g\|_q$. Over the real scalars the conjugation is omitted.
* **40. Reflexivity and weak compactness.** For $1<p<\infty$, $L^p$ is reflexive,
  so its bounded sets are relatively weakly compact. On a finite measure space,
  a subset of $L^1$ is relatively weakly compact exactly when it is uniformly
  integrable (Dunford–Pettis).
* **41. Continuity of translations.** For $f\in L^p(\mathbb R^n)$,
  $1\leq p<\infty$, $\|f(\cdot-h)-f\|_p\to0$ as $h\to0$.
* **42. Approximate identities.** If $\phi_\varepsilon\geq0$ have integral one
  and their mass outside every neighborhood of zero tends to zero, then
  $f*\phi_\varepsilon\to f$ in $L^p(\mathbb R^n)$ for $1\leq p<\infty$.
  For bounded uniformly continuous $f$, convergence is uniform.
* **43. Young's convolution inequality.** If $1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty$ and
  $1+1/r=1/p+1/q$, then
  $\|f*g\|_r\leq\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ on $\mathbb R^n$.

## Differentiation and Euclidean integration formulas

A **Lebesgue point** of $f\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb R^n)$ is a point $x$
at which the averages of $|f(y)-f(x)|$ over $B(x,r)$ tend to zero. A function on
$[a,b]$ is **absolutely continuous** if small total length of disjoint intervals
forces small total oscillation over their endpoints. It has **bounded variation**
if the sums of absolute increments over partitions are uniformly bounded.

* **44. Lebesgue differentiation.** Almost every point of a locally integrable
  function is a Lebesgue point. In particular,
  $m(B(x,r))^{-1}\int_{B(x,r)}f\,dm\to f(x)$ almost everywhere.
* **45. Density theorem.** For measurable $E\subseteq\mathbb R^n$,
  $m(E\cap B(x,r))/m(B(x,r))\to1_E(x)$ for almost every $x$.
* **46. Fundamental theorem of Lebesgue calculus.** A function $F$ on $[a,b]$
  is absolutely continuous exactly when
  $$F(x)=F(a)+\int_a^x g(t)\,dt\qquad(g\in L^1[a,b]).$$
  In this case $F'=g$ almost everywhere. Increasing functions, and hence functions
  of bounded variation, are differentiable almost everywhere; absolute continuity
  is the condition that excludes a singular part in their derivative measures.
* **47. Change of variables.** For a $C^1$ diffeomorphism $T:U\to V$ between
  open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$,
  $$\int_V f(y)\,dy=\int_U f(Tx)|\det DT(x)|\,dx$$
  for nonnegative measurable $f$ and for integrable $f$.
* **48. Polar integration.** With $\sigma$ the usual surface measure on
  $S^{n-1}$,
  $$\int_{\mathbb R^n}f(x)\,dx
    =\int_0^\infty\int_{S^{n-1}} f(r\omega)r^{n-1}\,d\sigma(\omega)\,dr$$
  for nonnegative measurable or integrable $f$.

## Radon measures, weak convergence, and structural extensions

A **Radon measure** here is a Borel measure on a Hausdorff space, finite on
compact sets and inner regular on Borel sets. On a metric space, probabilities
$\mu_n$ **converge weakly** to $\mu$ if
$\int f\,d\mu_n\to\int f\,d\mu$ for all bounded continuous $f$.
A family is **tight** if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, one compact set carries at
least $1-\varepsilon$ of every probability in the family.

Here $C_c(X)$ denotes continuous scalar functions of compact support, and
$C_0(X)$ denotes those vanishing at infinity: each set $\{|f|\geq\varepsilon\}$
is compact for $\varepsilon>0$. A positive functional takes nonnegative
functions to nonnegative numbers. A **Polish space** is a separable space with
a compatible complete metric; a **standard Borel space** is a measurable space
isomorphic to the Borel space of a Polish space.

* **49. Regularity on Polish spaces.** Every finite Borel measure on a Polish
  space is tight and Radon. In Euclidean space, measurable sets can be approximated
  from outside by open sets and finite-measure sets from inside by compact sets.
* **50. Riesz representation.** On a second-countable locally compact Hausdorff
  space, a positive
  linear functional on $C_c(X)$ is integration against a unique Radon measure.
  Here the representing measure can also be chosen outer regular on Borel sets;
  this is the usual regular-measure formulation of the theorem.
* **51. The dual of $C_0(X)$.** Every bounded linear functional on $C_0(X)$ is
  integration against a unique finite regular signed or complex Borel measure,
  and the functional norm is its total variation mass.
* **52. Portmanteau theorem.** For probabilities on a metric space, weak
  convergence is equivalent to
  $\limsup_n\mu_n(F)\leq\mu(F)$ for every closed $F$, or to
  $\liminf_n\mu_n(G)\geq\mu(G)$ for every open $G$. It implies convergence on
  Borel sets whose boundary has $\mu$-measure zero.
* **53. Prokhorov's theorem.** For probabilities on a Polish space, tightness
  is equivalent to relative compactness for weak convergence; equivalently,
  every sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence.
* **54. Daniell–Stone construction.** Let $L$ be a vector lattice of bounded
  real functions containing the constants. A positive linear functional $I$ on
  $L$ with $I(f_n)\downarrow0$ whenever $f_n\downarrow0$ pointwise is integration
  against a unique finite measure on $\sigma(L)$.
* **55. Haar measure.** Every locally compact Hausdorff group has a nonzero
  left-invariant regular Borel measure, finite on compact sets, unique up to a
  positive factor. Compact groups admit the normalization $\mu(G)=1$; abelian
  and compact groups are unimodular.
* **56. Strong measurability and Bochner integration.** A Banach-valued function
  is an almost-everywhere limit of measurable simple functions exactly when it
  is weakly measurable and essentially separably valued (Pettis's measurability
  theorem, on a complete measure space). Such a function is Bochner integrable
  exactly when $\int\|f\|\,d\mu<\infty$, and
  $\|\int f\,d\mu\|\leq\int\|f\|\,d\mu$.
* **57. Bochner convergence and functoriality.** Dominated convergence holds
  for strongly measurable Banach-valued functions with an integrable norm
  majorant. Bounded linear maps commute with the Bochner integral, and Fubini's
  theorem holds for Bochner-integrable functions on $\sigma$-finite products.

## Conditional integration, interpolation, and size of sets

For $1\leq p<\infty$, the **weak $L^p$ quasi-norm** is
$\|f\|_{p,\infty}=\sup_{t>0}t\mu\{|f|>t\}^{1/p}$.
A linear or sublinear operator has **weak type $(p,q)$** if
$\|Tf\|_{q,\infty}\leq C\|f\|_p$; strong type means the ordinary $L^q$ norm.

* **58. Conditional expectation.** On a probability space and for a
  sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal G$, every $f\in L^1$ has a unique
  $\mathcal G$-measurable conditional integral $\mathbb E[f\mid\mathcal G]$
  characterized by its integrals over sets in $\mathcal G$. It is positive,
  contractive on each $L^p$, and an orthogonal projection on $L^2$.
* **59. Disintegration.** A probability on a product of standard Borel spaces
  can be written $\mu(dx)K(x,dy)$, where $\mu$ is its first marginal and $K$ is
  a probability kernel unique $\mu$-almost everywhere. Integration can then be
  performed by first integrating against the conditional probability $K$.
* **60. De la Vallée Poussin criterion.** On a finite measure space,
  $\mathcal F\subseteq L^1$ is uniformly integrable exactly when there is an
  increasing convex $\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ with
  $\Phi(t)/t\to\infty$ and
  $\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\int\Phi(|f|)\,d\mu<\infty$.
* **61. Riesz–Thorin interpolation.** For complex $L^p$ spaces on
  $\sigma$-finite measure spaces, let a linear
  operator on $L^{p_0}+L^{p_1}$ have endpoint bounds
  $\|Tf\|_{q_j}\leq M_j\|f\|_{p_j}$, $j=0,1$, with exponents in $[1,\infty]$.
  If $0<\theta<1$ and
  $$p_\theta^{-1}=(1-\theta)p_0^{-1}+\theta p_1^{-1},\qquad
    q_\theta^{-1}=(1-\theta)q_0^{-1}+\theta q_1^{-1},$$
  then $\|Tf\|_{q_\theta}\leq M_0^{1-\theta}M_1^\theta\|f\|_{p_\theta}$.
* **62. Marcinkiewicz interpolation.** A sublinear operator of weak types
  $(p_0,p_0)$ and $(p_1,p_1)$, $1\leq p_0<p_1\leq\infty$, is bounded on
  $L^p$ for $p_0<p<p_1$; at the infinite endpoint the bound is an $L^\infty$
  bound. Constants depend only on the exponents and endpoint bounds. In
  particular, weak $(1,1)$ and strong $(\infty,\infty)$ imply strong $(p,p)$
  throughout $1<p<\infty$.
* **63. Hausdorff measures.** For $s>0$, the limit as $\delta\downarrow0$ of
  $$c_s\inf\left\{\sum_j(\operatorname{diam}E_j)^s:
      E\subseteq\bigcup_jE_j,\ \operatorname{diam}E_j\leq\delta\right\}$$
  defines a metric outer measure $\mathcal H^s$, so Borel sets are measurable.
  With the conventional normalization, $\mathcal H^n=\mathcal L^n$ on
  $\mathbb R^n$; for $s=0$ the measure is counting measure. An $L$-Lipschitz map
  satisfies $\mathcal H^s(f(E))\leq L^s\mathcal H^s(E)$ for $s>0$.
* **64. Hausdorff dimension.** The critical exponent
  $\dim_HE=\inf\{s:\mathcal H^s(E)=0\}$ is stable under countable unions:
  $\dim_H(\bigcup_jE_j)=\sup_j\dim_HE_j$. Lipschitz maps do not increase
  dimension, and bi-Lipschitz maps preserve it. A nonempty open subset of
  $\mathbb R^n$ has Hausdorff dimension $n$.

## Reference guide

The principal overlap is Folland, *Real Analysis*, Chapters 1–3 and 6–7, and
Cohn, *Measure Theory*, Chapters 1–7. These give the construction, convergence,
product, density, and $L^p$ results above. Stein–Shakarchi, *Real Analysis*,
Chapters 1–3 and 6, provides an independent Euclidean treatment, especially of
approximation and differentiation. The corresponding material also organizes
the graduate courses of Rudin, Royden–Fitzpatrick, Halmos, Axler, and Schilling
listed in the bibliographies.

For the structural end of the synopsis, Cohn, Chapters 7–9 and Appendix E,
and Folland, Chapters 7 and 11, connect regular measures, Haar measure, and
vector integration with the larger accounts in Bogachev, Fremlin, and Bourbaki.
Weak convergence is developed further in Bogachev's *Measure Theory* and
*Weak Convergence of Measures*, and in Parthasarathy. These references supply
broader variants; the statements here retain a common usable setting.

The interpolation results are treated in Folland, Chapter 6; Hausdorff measures
and dimension in Folland, Chapter 11, and Stein–Shakarchi, Chapter 7.
Conditional integration and disintegration connect Cohn, Chapters 8 and 10,
with Bogachev's structural account. The uniform-integrability criterion is also
part of the recurring measure/probability interface.
