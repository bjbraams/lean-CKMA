# Core Knowledge in Mathematical Analysis

## A supplementary bibliography of recent books

This file supplements `CKMA-bib.md`. It uses the same fifty-five sections.
An entry belongs here when it is a graduate textbook or a research
monograph, chiefly published since about 2010, and when it is not already
listed in the classics bibliography. The year 2010 is a working boundary,
not a rule. A smaller number of books from roughly 2004–2009 are included
when they are still the working monograph of their topic and the classics
list does not already carry them.

These books do not have the status of the classics list. Many are the
current textbook or the current research synthesis of a subject that has
moved since the older references were written. Others are substantial
monographs that a specialist actually consults, without yet being part of
the common language of the whole field. The selection favours books from
the American Mathematical Society, Springer, Birkhäuser, the European
Mathematical Society, Cambridge University Press, Oxford University Press,
Princeton University Press, and a few other houses with the same role.
Lecture notes are included mainly when they have stabilised as books;
one substantial, freely available graduate course is also included.

Years and publishers are given because recency is the point of the list.
Edition and translation dates are distinguished from first publication
when relevant. Links on publishers lead to book records, usually through
DOIs; an author page or the notes themselves is used for online resources.
Cross-listing is used more sparingly than in `CKMA-bib.md`. A book that
serves two sections usually appears in full once, with a short pointer
from the other section.

The list is smaller than the classics bibliography, and it is not a
ranking. Areas whose research literature is still mainly in papers are
represented by the nearest stable monograph rather than by an article list.
Quiet sections are short on purpose.

# Part I. Panoramas, History, and Foundations

---

## 1. Broad Courses, Encyclopedias, and Maps of Analysis

The comprehensive courses and maps in the classics list still organise the
subject. What has appeared since is a handful of broad graduate books and
one applied companion to the Princeton Companion, together with two
multi-volume courses from the German and Russian traditions.

* **Nicholas J. Higham (ed.), *The Princeton Companion to Applied
  Mathematics*** (2015, [Princeton University Press](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400874477)). The applied
  counterpart of the Princeton Companion: a map of how analysis is used,
  written in short signed articles.
* **Roger Godement, *Analysis I–IV*** (Springer; [I](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-18491-8), [II](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-29926-2), [III](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-16053-5), [IV](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-16907-1); French editions
  2001–2003, English translations through 2015). A late Bourbaki-school
  *cours d'analyse*, slow, historically commented, and wide enough to
  function as a panorama rather than as a single course.
* **Terence Tao, *An Epsilon of Room, I: Real Analysis*** (2010, [American
  Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/gsm/117), Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A second
  graduate pass through real analysis, Hilbert space, and Fourier
  analysis, written as a bridge out of a first course. Volume II is a
  separate collection of expository notes.
* **D. J. H. Garling, *A Course in Mathematical Analysis*** (3 volumes,
  2013–2014, Cambridge University Press; [I](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139424493), [II](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139424509), [III](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139424516)). A broad English-style graduate
  course from foundations through complex analysis and measure.
* **Manfred Einsiedler and Thomas Ward, *Functional Analysis, Spectral
  Theory, and Applications*** (2017, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-58540-6), Graduate Texts in
  Mathematics). Listed in full in Section 15. It is one of the few recent
  single books that a student can read across functional analysis and
  spectral theory.
* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-14205-5), Graduate Texts in Mathematics). Listed in
  full in Section 6. A modern panorama of the measure-theoretic route into
  probability.
* **Jean-Paul Penot, *Analysis: From Concepts to Applications*** (2016,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-32411-1)). A broad course
  joining real and functional analysis with differential calculus, convexity,
  and applications. Useful for readers who want these tools developed within
  one framework.
* **Denis Choimet and Hervé Queffélec, *Twelve Landmarks of Twentieth-Century
  Analysis*** (2015; expanded English translation of the 2009 French book,
  [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107445017)).
  Complete proofs and historical commentary around major theorems, including
  the corona problem, Littlewood's conjecture, and the Banach–Tarski paradox.
  A second course organised around mathematical ideas.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev and Oleg G. Smolyanov, *Real and Functional
  Analysis*** (2020; expanded English translation,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-38219-3)). A substantial
  university course covering measure, integration, functional analysis, and
  distributions. More structural than a first real-analysis text, with
  connections to infinite-dimensional analysis.
* **Herbert Amann and Joachim Escher, *Analysis I–III*** (English
  translation 2005–2009, [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/b137107); [volume II](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-7643-7478-5), [volume III](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-7643-7480-8)). A
  rigorous three-volume course from foundations through manifolds, Lebesgue
  integration, and Stokes's theorem in a general setting. Its systematic use
  of Banach-space language makes it a natural bridge to graduate analysis.
* **Vladimir A. Zorich, *Mathematical Analysis I–II*** (2nd English ed.,
  2015–2016, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-48792-1); [volume II](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-48993-2)). The
  Moscow analysis course, from the real numbers through differential forms,
  Fourier series, and asymptotic methods, with physical motivation
  throughout. Advanced undergraduate in level, but often used as a graduate
  reference for the classical material.

## 2. History, Perspective, and Essays

The classics list carries the standard histories of functional analysis,
integration, and Banach spaces. The recent books are mostly histories of
nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century analysis, written so that
the mathematics remains visible, together with the Scottish Book and a
few expository collections.

* **Jeremy Gray, *[The Real and the Complex: A History of Analysis in the
  19th Century](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-23715-2)*** (2015, Oxford University Press). The broadest recent
  history of the century in which real and complex analysis separated.
* **Umberto Bottazzini and Jeremy Gray, *Hidden Harmony — Geometric
  Fantasies: The Rise of Complex Function Theory*** (2013, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-5725-1)). A
  large history of complex analysis from its eighteenth-century roots
  through the early twentieth century.
* **Ranjan Roy, *Sources in the Development of Mathematics: Infinite
  Series and Products from the Fifteenth to the Twenty-first Century***
  (2011, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511844195)). Series, products, and special
  functions read through the original arguments, with the mathematics
  done rather than summarised.
* **Ranjan Roy, *Elliptic and Modular Functions from Gauss to Dedekind to
  Hecke*** (2017, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316671504)). A source-driven history of
  elliptic and modular functions that is also a usable introduction to
  the classical theory.
* **Jeremy Gray, *Henri Poincaré: A Scientific Biography*** (2013,
  [Princeton University Press](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400844791)). Poincaré's analysis, dynamics, and
  mathematical physics, treated as one scientific life.
* **Michèle Audin, *Fatou, Julia, Montel: The Great Prize of Mathematical
  Sciences of 1918, and Beyond*** (2011, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-17854-2)). The iteration-theory
  prize of 1918, and the formation of complex dynamics, reconstructed
  from the record.
* **Michèle Audin, *Remembering Sofya Kovalevskaya*** (2011, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-85729-929-1)). A
  scientific biography of Kovalevskaya, useful for the nineteenth-century
  analytic milieu around Weierstrass.
* **Daniel S. Alexander, Felice Iavernaro, and Alessandro Rosa, *Early
  Days of Complex Dynamics: A History of Complex Dynamics in One Variable
  During 1906–1942*** (2012, [American Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/hmath/038)). The history
  between Fatou–Julia and the modern revival.
* **Reinhard Siegmund-Schultze, *Mathematicians Fleeing from Nazi Germany:
  Individual Fates and the Global Impact*** (2009, [Princeton University
  Press](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400831401)). How the analytic schools of the 1930s were physically
  redistributed.
* **Reuben Hersh, *Peter Lax, Mathematician: An Illustrated Memoir***
  (2015, [American Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/mbk/088)). A scientific memoir of a career
  that runs through functional analysis, PDE, and computation.
* **Hans Niels Jahnke (ed.), *A History of Analysis*** (2003, [American
  Mathematical Society](https://www.ams.org/books/hmath/024/)). A collective history from antiquity to the
  twentieth century, still the broadest single volume of its kind.
* **David Aubin and Catherine Goldstein (eds.), *The War of Guns and
  Mathematics: Mathematical Practices and Communities in France and Its
  Western Allies around World War I*** (2014, [American Mathematical
  Society](https://www.ams.org/books/hmath/043/)). French analysis and its institutions in the 1914–1918 war.
* **Paolo Freguglia and Mariano Giaquinta, *The Early Period of the Calculus
  of Variations*** (2016,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-38945-5)). The development
  from geometric extremal problems to Euler and Lagrange, with the original
  mathematics reconstructed. A history for readers who want to understand the
  formation of variational methods.
* **Laurent Mazliak and Glenn Shafer (eds.), *The Splendors and Miseries of
  Martingales: Their History from the Casino to Mathematics*** (2022,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-05988-9)). A focused
  historical collection tracing gambling strategies, probability, and the
  emergence of the martingale concept. Complements the technical
  stochastic-calculus books with their intellectual history.
* **David M. Bressoud, *A Radical Approach to Lebesgue's Theory of
  Integration*** (2008, [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/9780521711838) and Mathematical
  Association of America). The road from Fourier series and Riemann
  integration to Lebesgue's theory, told through the problems that forced
  each step. A history-driven course rather than a history.
* **R. Daniel Mauldin (ed.), *The Scottish Book: Mathematics from the
  Scottish Café*** (2nd ed., 2015,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-22897-6)). The problems of
  the Lwów school with commentaries on their later history. Many of them are
  problems of Banach-space theory and real analysis that are still cited.
* **Terence Tao, *Compactness and Contradiction*** (2013, [American
  Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/mbk/081)). Expository essays
  on compactness, ultrafilters, and the correspondence between finitary and
  infinitary arguments in analysis and combinatorics. A companion in spirit
  to *An Epsilon of Room*.

## 3. Set-Theoretic and Descriptive Foundations for Analysts

Kechris, Moschovakis, and Gao remain the core. The recent books are about
Polish groups, classification, and the descriptive set theory that
operator algebras and dynamics now use.

* **Alexander S. Kechris, *Global Aspects of Ergodic Group Actions***
  (2010, [American Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/surv/160), Mathematical Surveys and
  Monographs). Countable Borel equivalence relations and the global
  structure of ergodic actions, written for both logicians and dynamicists.
* **Christian Rosendal, *Coarse Geometry of Topological Groups*** (2021,
  [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108903547)). Polish groups, coarse structure, and the
  descriptive theory of large topological groups.
* **Stevo Todorčević, *Introduction to Ramsey Spaces*** (2010, [Princeton
  University Press](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400835409)). Ramsey theory in the form used for Banach spaces,
  topological dynamics, and classification.
* **Pandelis Dodos, *Banach Spaces and Descriptive Set Theory: Selected
  Topics*** (2010, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-12153-1), Lecture Notes in Mathematics). The
  descriptive complexity of Banach-space classification problems.
* **Vladimir Pestov, *Dynamics of Infinite-dimensional Groups: The
  Ramsey–Dvoretzky–Milman Phenomenon*** (2006, [American Mathematical
  Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/ulect/040)). Extreme amenability and concentration, at the meeting point
  of Polish groups and geometric functional analysis.
* **Ilijas Farah, *Combinatorial Set Theory of C\*-algebras*** (2019,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-27093-3)). Listed in full in Section 23. Set theory as a working tool
  inside operator algebras.
* **Matthew Foreman and Akihiro Kanamori (eds.), *Handbook of Set
  Theory*** (2010, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4020-5764-9)). The large reference when an analytic
  question has become a set-theoretic one.
* **Lorenz J. Halbeisen, *Combinatorial Set Theory, with a Gentle
  Introduction to Forcing*** (2nd ed., 2017, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-60231-8)). A readable route
  into forcing and infinitary combinatorics for readers who are not
  logicians first.
* **Ralf Schindler, *Set Theory: Exploring Independence and Truth***
  (2014, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-06725-4)). A modern graduate account of forcing and inner
  models, shorter than Jech.
* **Alexander S. Kechris and Benjamin D. Miller, *Topics in Orbit
  Equivalence*** (2004, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/b99421), Lecture
  Notes in Mathematics). Countable Borel equivalence relations, amenability,
  treeability, and cost. The standard short introduction before Kechris's
  later monograph.

## 4. Real Analysis, Measure, and Integration

Folland, Bogachev, and Fremlin still anchor the subject. The recent
books are courses, counterexample collections, and the theory of weak
convergence.

* **Sheldon Axler, *Measure, Integration & Real Analysis*** (2020,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-33143-6), Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A clean graduate course,
  freely available, with the functional analysis developed far enough
  for a first pass at spectral theory.
* **René L. Schilling, *Measures, Integrals and Martingales*** (2nd ed.,
  2017, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316718339)). A careful first graduate book that
  reaches martingales without assuming a probability course.
* **René L. Schilling and Franziska Kühn, *Counterexamples in Measure
  and Integration*** (2021, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009003797)). The modern
  companion to Gelbaum–Olmsted for measure-theoretic pathology. Listed
  again in Section 54.
* **D. J. H. Garling, *A Course in Mathematical Analysis***, volumes II
  and III (2013–2014, Cambridge University Press; [II](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139424509), [III](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139424516)). Measure, integration,
  and functional analysis inside a three-volume course.
* **Christopher Heil, *Introduction to Real Analysis*** (2019, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-26903-6),
  Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A long graduate course strong on the
  concrete real-variable material that shorter books compress.
* **Emmanuele DiBenedetto, *Real Analysis*** (2nd ed., 2016,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4939-4005-9)). A PDE analyst's real analysis: measure, weak derivatives,
  and the facts used in regularity theory.
* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Essentials of Integration Theory for Analysis***
  (2011, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-1135-2), Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A short, sharp
  integration course written for later use in probability and PDE.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Weak Convergence of Measures*** (2018,
  [American Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/surv/234), Mathematical Surveys and Monographs).
  The research reference for weak convergence, Prokhorov theory, and
  the infinite-dimensional case, beyond the chapters in Bogachev's
  *Measure Theory*.
* **Richard L. Wheeden and Antoni Zygmund, *Measure and Integral: An
  Introduction to Real Analysis*** (2nd ed., 2015, [CRC Press](https://doi.org/10.1201/b18361)). A
  concrete Euclidean development, useful where abstract treatments skip
  the covering arguments.
* **Richard F. Bass, *[Real Analysis for Graduate Students](https://r-bass.scholar.uconn.edu/)*** (2nd ed.,
  2013; freely available, with later printings). A compact graduate
  course used for self-study, covering measure, functional analysis,
  and a first treatment of distributions and Sobolev spaces.
* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-14205-5)). Listed in full in Section 6.
* **Hari Bercovici, Arlen Brown, and Carl Pearcy, *Measure and Integration***
  (2016, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-29046-1)). An
  introductory graduate course in Lebesgue integration, convergence, and
  function spaces. Particularly useful as preparation for these authors'
  operator-theoretic approach to functional analysis.
* **Piermarco Cannarsa and Teresa D'Aprile, *Introduction to Measure Theory
  and Functional Analysis*** (2015; English translation of the 2008 Italian
  edition, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-17019-0)). Measure and
  integration followed by Banach and Hilbert spaces, weak convergence, and
  compact operators. A compact route to the functional analysis used in
  differential equations.
* **William P. Ziemer, *Modern Real Analysis*** (2nd ed., 2017, with
  contributions by Monica Torres,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-64629-9)). A graduate course
  combining measure and integration with differentiation and functional
  analysis. Distinct from Ziemer's specialised *Weakly Differentiable
  Functions* in the classics list.
* **Sergei Ovchinnikov, *Measure, Integral, Derivative: A Course on Lebesgue's
  Theory*** (2013, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-7196-7)). A
  concentrated account of Lebesgue theory and differentiation. Suitable for a
  graduate bridge course when the broader functional-analysis syllabus is
  being taught separately.
* **Boris M. Makarov and Anatolii N. Podkorytov, *Real Analysis: Measures,
  Integrals and Applications*** (2013; English translation,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4471-5122-7)). A detailed course
  with substantial examples and applications of integration. The emphasis on
  concrete arguments makes it a useful supplement to more compressed graduate
  texts.

## 5. Differentiation and the Fine Structure of Real Functions

This remains a classical subject. The recent books treat smooth calculus, bounded variation,
the implicit function theorem, pathological functions,
differentiability in infinite dimensions, and the divergence theorem for
rough sets.

* **Joram Lindenstrauss, David Preiss, and Jaroslav Tišer, *Fréchet
  Differentiability of Lipschitz Functions and Porous Sets in Banach
  Spaces*** (2012, [Princeton University Press](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400842698), Annals of Mathematics
  Studies). The definitive account of Fréchet differentiability of
  Lipschitz functions on Banach spaces, and of the porous sets that
  obstruct it.
* **Washek F. Pfeffer, *The Divergence Theorem and Sets of Finite
  Perimeter*** (2012, [CRC Press](https://doi.org/10.1201/b11919)). The Gauss–Green theorem on rough
  sets, written from the real-variable side.
* **Pertti Mattila, *Rectifiability: A Survey*** (2023, [Cambridge
  University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009288057), London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series).
  Listed in full in Section 37. Densities, tangents, and the fine
  structure of measures.
* **Stanislav Hencl and Pekka Koskela, *Lectures on Mappings of Finite
  Distortion*** (2014, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-03173-6), Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Listed
  in full in Section 44. Differentiability and distortion in the
  Sobolev category.
* **Elijah Liflyand, *Functions of Bounded Variation and Their Fourier
  Transforms*** (2019,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-04429-9)). Variation in one
  and several variables, integrability of Fourier transforms, and summability.
  A research synthesis connecting fine real-variable properties with harmonic
  analysis.
* **Boris M. Makarov and Anatolii N. Podkorytov, *Smooth Functions and Maps***
  (2021; English translation,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-79438-5)). Smooth calculus in
  finite dimensions, including inverse and implicit functions, critical
  points, and singularities. Useful background for analysts entering
  differential topology and geometric problems.
* **Steven G. Krantz and Harold R. Parks, *The Implicit Function Theorem:
  History, Theory, and Applications*** (2002; reprinted 2013,
  [Birkhäuser](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-5981-1)). The implicit
  function theorem in its many forms, from the classical and nonsmooth
  versions to Nash–Moser. Historical and technical at once.
* **Marek Jarnicki and Peter Pflug, *Continuous Nowhere Differentiable
  Functions: The Monsters of Analysis*** (2015,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-12670-8)). The constructions
  of Weierstrass, Takagi, and their successors, with differentiability,
  Hölder, and typicality properties proved in full.
* **Alexander Kharazishvili, *Strange Functions in Real Analysis*** (3rd
  ed., 2017, [CRC Press](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315154473)).
  Pathological functions, among them Sierpiński–Zygmund functions,
  nonmeasurable functions, and Hamel-basis constructions, together with the
  set theory behind them. Complements Gelbaum–Olmsted with proofs.

## 6. Probability: Measure-Theoretic Foundations and Limit Theory

Durrett, Kallenberg, and Billingsley remain the standards. The recent
books are new graduate courses, the limit theory of processes on
graphs, trees, and groups, point processes, and the rigorous
statistical mechanics of lattice systems and spin glasses.

* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-14205-5), Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A
  graduate course from measure theory to martingales, Markov chains,
  and Brownian motion, with the analytic connections kept in view.
* **Erhan Çinlar, *Probability and Stochastics*** (2011, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-87859-1),
  Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A broad graduate text in the
  Strasbourg tradition, strong on conditioning and processes.
* **Allan Gut, *Probability: A Graduate Course*** (2nd ed., 2013,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-4708-5)). A systematic course in limit theorems, characteristic
  functions, and convergence.
* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Mathematics of Probability*** (2013, [American
  Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/gsm/149), Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A short
  analyst's course, distinct from Stroock's *Probability Theory: An
  Analytic View*.
* **Firas Rassoul-Agha and Timo Seppäläinen, *A Course on Large
  Deviations with an Introduction to Gibbs Measures*** (2015, [American
  Mathematical Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/gsm/162), Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A modern
  graduate route into large deviations, closer to statistical mechanics
  than Dembo–Zeitouni.
* **S. R. S. Varadhan, *Large Deviations*** (2016, [American Mathematical
  Society](https://doi.org/10.1090/cln/027), Courant Lecture Notes). Varadhan's own short account of the
  theory he founded.
* **Andreas E. Kyprianou, *Fluctuations of Lévy Processes with
  Applications*** (2nd ed., 2014, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-37632-0)). Path decomposition, exit
  problems, and the fluctuation theory that Sato's treatise does not
  emphasise.
* **David Applebaum, *Lévy Processes and Stochastic Calculus*** (2nd ed.,
  2009, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511809781)). Stochastic integration for
  processes with jumps, the usual companion to the pathwise theory.
* **Gregory F. Lawler and Vlada Limic, *Random Walk: A Modern
  Introduction*** (2010, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511750854)). Potential theory,
  intersection, and the heat kernel for random walk, written for
  analysts.
* **Russell Lyons and Yuval Peres, *Probability on Trees and Networks***
  (2016, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316672815)). Branching processes, percolation,
  uniform spanning trees, and random walk on graphs, in one systematic
  book.
* **Geoffrey Grimmett, *Probability on Graphs: Random Processes on
  Graphs and Lattices*** (2nd ed., 2018, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108528986)). A
  short graduate course on percolation, random-cluster models, and
  related processes.
* **Remco van der Hofstad, *Random Graphs and Complex Networks***,
  volume I (2017) and volume II (2024, Cambridge University Press; [I](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316779422), [II](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316795552)). The
  current graduate reference for random graphs, from local weak
  convergence to mesoscopic structure.
* **Olav Kallenberg, *Random Measures, Theory and Applications*** (2017,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-41598-7)). The research treatise on random measures and point
  processes, separate from Kallenberg's *Foundations*.
* **Henry P. McKean, *Probability: The Classical Limit Theorems*** (2014,
  [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107282032)). A
  concise, distinctive treatment of laws of large numbers, central limit
  behaviour, and Brownian motion. An experienced probabilist's account of the
  analytic mechanisms behind the limit theorems.
* **Santosh S. Venkatesh, *The Theory of Probability: Explorations and
  Applications*** (2013, [Cambridge University
  Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139169325)). A substantial course
  coupling proofs with historical context and detailed applications.
  Especially useful for moving between classical probability, asymptotics, and
  probabilistic reasoning in analysis.
* **Louis H. Y. Chen, Larry Goldstein, and Qi-Man Shao, *Normal Approximation
  by Stein's Method*** (2011,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15007-4)). A systematic
  research reference for quantitative normal approximation, including
  dependent variables. Develops Stein's method far beyond the elementary
  central limit theorem.
* **Vydas Čekanavičius, *Approximation Methods in Probability Theory*** (2016,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-34072-2)). Distributional

  Poisson and compound Poisson approximation. A useful complement to books

* **Oleg Klesov, *Limit Theorems for Multi-Indexed Sums of Random Variables***
  (2014, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-44388-0)). Strong laws,

* **Gennady Samorodnitsky, *Stochastic Processes and Long Range Dependence***
  (2016, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-45575-4)). Long memory,

  problems.
* **Sébastien Roch, *Modern Discrete Probability: An Essential Toolkit***
  (2024, [Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781009305129)).

* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Gaussian Measures in Finite and Infinite Dimensions***
  (2023, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-23122-3)). A graduate

  Complements Bogachev's research treatise with a more compact course.
* **Günter Last and Mathew Penrose, *Lectures on the Poisson Process***
  (2017, [Cambridge University
  Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316104477)). Poisson point processes on
  general spaces: the Mecke equation, Fock-space representation, Poincaré
  inequalities, and normal approximation. The modern compact reference.
* **D. J. Daley and D. Vere-Jones, *An Introduction to the Theory of Point
  Processes*** (2 volumes, 2nd ed., 2003–2008,
  [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-49835-5); [volume I](https://doi.org/10.1007/b97277)). The standard
  reference on point processes and random measures, from elementary models
  to Palm theory and convergence. The link is to volume II.
* **Michel Talagrand, *Mean Field Models for Spin Glasses*** (2 volumes,
  2011, [Springer](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15202-3); [volume II](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-22253-5)). The rigorous
  theory of the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model and its relatives, including

* **Dmitry Panchenko, *The Sherrington–Kirkpatrick Model*** (2013,

* **Sacha Friedli and Yvan Velenik, *Statistical Mechanics of Lattice

  University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781316882603)). Ising, Potts,

* **Hans-Otto Georgii, *Gibbs Measures and Phase Transitions*** (2nd ed.,

# How to use this list beside the classics bibliography

Read `CKMA-bib.md` for the books that organised a subject, and read this
file for a later textbook or a later research synthesis. In several
sections the later synthesis is already in the classics list, and this
file is then short. That is deliberate. Examples are rough paths, the
vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund theory, optimal transport on metric
measure spaces, and the decoupling theorem: the books that play that
role are already listed there.

A book in this file is not a claim that it has replaced a classic. It is
a claim that a graduate student or a specialist can usefully open it
now, and that knowing it exists is worth more than searching a
publisher's catalogue from scratch.

The same boundaries apply as in the classics list. Numerical analysis,
statistics, and analytic number theory appear only where the book is
also a book of analysis. Areas whose literature is still a sequence of
papers are represented by the nearest stable monograph, or omitted.
