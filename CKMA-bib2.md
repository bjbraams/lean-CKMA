# Core Knowledge in Mathematical Analysis

## A supplementary bibliography of recent books

This file supplements `CKMA-bib.md`. It uses the same fifty-five sections.
An entry belongs here when it is a graduate textbook or a research
monograph, chiefly published since about 2010, and when it is not already
listed in the classics bibliography. The year 2010 is a working boundary,
not a rule. A smaller number of books from roughly 2004–2009 are included
when they are still the working monograph of their topic and the classics
list does not already carry them.

These books do not have the status of the classics list. Many are the
current textbook or the current research synthesis of a subject that has
moved since the older references were written. Others are substantial
monographs that a specialist actually consults, without yet being part of
the common language of the whole field. The selection favours books from
the American Mathematical Society, Springer, Birkhäuser, the European
Mathematical Society, Cambridge University Press, Oxford University Press,
Princeton University Press, and a few other houses with the same role.
Lecture notes are included only when they have stabilised as books.

Years and publishers are given because recency is the point of the list.
Cross-listing is used more sparingly than in `CKMA-bib.md`. A book that
serves two sections usually appears in full once, with a short pointer
from the other section.

The list is smaller than the classics bibliography, and it is not a
ranking. Areas whose research literature is still mainly in papers are
represented by the nearest stable monograph rather than by an article list.
Quiet sections are short on purpose.

# Part I. Panoramas, History, and Foundations

---

## 1. Broad Courses, Encyclopedias, and Maps of Analysis

The comprehensive courses and maps in the classics list still organise the
subject. What has appeared since is a handful of broad graduate books and
one applied companion to the Princeton Companion.

* **Nicholas J. Higham (ed.), *The Princeton Companion to Applied
  Mathematics*** (2015, Princeton University Press). The applied
  counterpart of the Princeton Companion: a map of how analysis is used,
  written in short signed articles.
* **Roger Godement, *Analysis I–IV*** (Springer; French editions
  2001–2003, English translations through 2015). A late Bourbaki-school
  *cours d'analyse*, slow, historically commented, and wide enough to
  function as a panorama rather than as a single course.
* **Terence Tao, *An Epsilon of Room, I: Real Analysis*** (2010, American
  Mathematical Society, Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A second
  graduate pass through real analysis, Hilbert space, and Fourier
  analysis, written as a bridge out of a first course. Volume II is a
  separate collection of expository notes.
* **D. J. H. Garling, *A Course in Mathematical Analysis*** (3 volumes,
  2013–2014, Cambridge University Press). A broad English-style graduate
  course from foundations through complex analysis and measure.
* **Manfred Einsiedler and Thomas Ward, *Functional Analysis, Spectral
  Theory, and Applications*** (2017, Springer, Graduate Texts in
  Mathematics). Listed in full in Section 15. It is one of the few recent
  single books that a student can read across functional analysis and
  spectral theory.
* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, Springer, Graduate Texts in Mathematics). Listed in
  full in Section 6. A modern panorama of the measure-theoretic route into
  probability.

## 2. History, Perspective, and Essays

The classics list carries the standard histories of functional analysis,
integration, and Banach spaces. The recent books are mostly histories of
nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century analysis, written so that
the mathematics remains visible.

* **Jeremy Gray, *The Real and the Complex: A History of Analysis in the
  19th Century*** (2015, Oxford University Press). The broadest recent
  history of the century in which real and complex analysis separated.
* **Umberto Bottazzini and Jeremy Gray, *Hidden Harmony — Geometric
  Fantasies: The Rise of Complex Function Theory*** (2013, Springer). A
  large history of complex analysis from its eighteenth-century roots
  through the early twentieth century.
* **Ranjan Roy, *Sources in the Development of Mathematics: Infinite
  Series and Products from the Fifteenth to the Twenty-first Century***
  (2011, Cambridge University Press). Series, products, and special
  functions read through the original arguments, with the mathematics
  done rather than summarised.
* **Ranjan Roy, *Elliptic and Modular Functions from Gauss to Dedekind to
  Hecke*** (2017, Cambridge University Press). A source-driven history of
  elliptic and modular functions that is also a usable introduction to
  the classical theory.
* **Jeremy Gray, *Henri Poincaré: A Scientific Biography*** (2013,
  Princeton University Press). Poincaré's analysis, dynamics, and
  mathematical physics, treated as one scientific life.
* **Michèle Audin, *Fatou, Julia, Montel: The Great Prize of Mathematical
  Sciences of 1918, and Beyond*** (2011, Springer). The iteration-theory
  prize of 1918, and the formation of complex dynamics, reconstructed
  from the record.
* **Michèle Audin, *Remembering Sofya Kovalevskaya*** (2011, Springer). A
  scientific biography of Kovalevskaya, useful for the nineteenth-century
  analytic milieu around Weierstrass.
* **Daniel S. Alexander, Felice Iavernaro, and Alessandro Rosa, *Early
  Days of Complex Dynamics: A History of Complex Dynamics in One Variable
  During 1906–1942*** (2012, American Mathematical Society). The history
  between Fatou–Julia and the modern revival.
* **Reinhard Siegmund-Schultze, *Mathematicians Fleeing from Nazi Germany:
  Individual Fates and the Global Impact*** (2009, Princeton University
  Press). How the analytic schools of the 1930s were physically
  redistributed.
* **Reuben Hersh, *Peter Lax, Mathematician: An Illustrated Memoir***
  (2015, American Mathematical Society). A scientific memoir of a career
  that runs through functional analysis, PDE, and computation.
* **Hans Niels Jahnke (ed.), *A History of Analysis*** (2003, American
  Mathematical Society). A collective history from antiquity to the
  twentieth century, still the broadest single volume of its kind.
* **David Aubin and Catherine Goldstein (eds.), *The War of Guns and
  Mathematics: Mathematical Practices and Communities in France and Its
  Western Allies around World War I*** (2014, American Mathematical
  Society). French analysis and its institutions in the 1914–1918 war.

## 3. Set-Theoretic and Descriptive Foundations for Analysts

Kechris, Moschovakis, and Gao remain the core. The recent books are about
Polish groups, classification, and the descriptive set theory that
operator algebras and dynamics now use.

* **Alexander S. Kechris, *Global Aspects of Ergodic Group Actions***
  (2010, American Mathematical Society, Mathematical Surveys and
  Monographs). Countable Borel equivalence relations and the global
  structure of ergodic actions, written for both logicians and dynamicists.
* **Christian Rosendal, *Coarse Geometry of Topological Groups*** (2021,
  Cambridge University Press). Polish groups, coarse structure, and the
  descriptive theory of large topological groups.
* **Stevo Todorčević, *Introduction to Ramsey Spaces*** (2010, Princeton
  University Press). Ramsey theory in the form used for Banach spaces,
  topological dynamics, and classification.
* **Pandelis Dodos, *Banach Spaces and Descriptive Set Theory: Selected
  Topics*** (2010, Springer, Lecture Notes in Mathematics). The
  descriptive complexity of Banach-space classification problems.
* **Vladimir Pestov, *Dynamics of Infinite-dimensional Groups: The
  Ramsey–Dvoretzky–Milman Phenomenon*** (2006, American Mathematical
  Society). Extreme amenability and concentration, at the meeting point
  of Polish groups and geometric functional analysis.
* **Ilijas Farah, *Combinatorial Set Theory of C\*-algebras*** (2019,
  Springer). Listed in full in Section 23. Set theory as a working tool
  inside operator algebras.
* **Matthew Foreman and Akihiro Kanamori (eds.), *Handbook of Set
  Theory*** (2010, Springer). The large reference when an analytic
  question has become a set-theoretic one.
* **Lorenz J. Halbeisen, *Combinatorial Set Theory, with a Gentle
  Introduction to Forcing*** (2nd ed., 2017, Springer). A readable route
  into forcing and infinitary combinatorics for readers who are not
  logicians first.
* **Ralf Schindler, *Set Theory: Exploring Independence and Truth***
  (2014, Springer). A modern graduate account of forcing and inner
  models, shorter than Jech.

# Part II. Real Analysis, Measure, and Probability

---

## 4. Real Analysis, Measure, and Integration

Folland, Bogachev, and Fremlin still anchor the subject. The recent
books are courses, counterexample collections, and the theory of weak
convergence.

* **Sheldon Axler, *Measure, Integration & Real Analysis*** (2020,
  Springer, Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A clean graduate course,
  freely available, with the functional analysis developed far enough
  for a first pass at spectral theory.
* **René L. Schilling, *Measures, Integrals and Martingales*** (2nd ed.,
  2017, Cambridge University Press). A careful first graduate book that
  reaches martingales without assuming a probability course.
* **René L. Schilling and Franziska Kühn, *Counterexamples in Measure
  and Integration*** (2021, Cambridge University Press). The modern
  companion to Gelbaum–Olmsted for measure-theoretic pathology. Listed
  again in Section 54.
* **D. J. H. Garling, *A Course in Mathematical Analysis***, volumes II
  and III (2013–2014, Cambridge University Press). Measure, integration,
  and functional analysis inside a three-volume course.
* **Christopher Heil, *Introduction to Real Analysis*** (2019, Springer,
  Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A long graduate course strong on the
  concrete real-variable material that shorter books compress.
* **Emmanuele DiBenedetto, *Real Analysis*** (2nd ed., 2016,
  Birkhäuser). A PDE analyst's real analysis: measure, weak derivatives,
  and the facts used in regularity theory.
* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Essentials of Integration Theory for Analysis***
  (2011, Springer, Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A short, sharp
  integration course written for later use in probability and PDE.
* **Vladimir I. Bogachev, *Weak Convergence of Measures*** (2018,
  American Mathematical Society, Mathematical Surveys and Monographs).
  The research reference for weak convergence, Prokhorov theory, and
  the infinite-dimensional case, beyond the chapters in Bogachev's
  *Measure Theory*.
* **Richard L. Wheeden and Antoni Zygmund, *Measure and Integral: An
  Introduction to Real Analysis*** (2nd ed., 2015, CRC Press). A
  concrete Euclidean development, useful where abstract treatments skip
  the covering arguments.
* **Richard F. Bass, *Real Analysis for Graduate Students*** (2nd ed.,
  2013; freely available, with later printings). A compact graduate
  course used for self-study, covering measure, functional analysis,
  and a first treatment of distributions and Sobolev spaces.
* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, Springer). Listed in full in Section 6.

## 5. Differentiation and the Fine Structure of Real Functions

This remains a classical subject. The important recent monographs are
about differentiability in infinite dimensions and about the divergence
theorem for rough sets.

* **Joram Lindenstrauss, David Preiss, and Jaroslav Tišer, *Fréchet
  Differentiability of Lipschitz Functions and Porous Sets in Banach
  Spaces*** (2012, Princeton University Press, Annals of Mathematics
  Studies). The definitive account of Fréchet differentiability of
  Lipschitz functions on Banach spaces, and of the porous sets that
  obstruct it.
* **Washek F. Pfeffer, *The Divergence Theorem and Sets of Finite
  Perimeter*** (2012, CRC Press). The Gauss–Green theorem on rough
  sets, written from the real-variable side.
* **Pertti Mattila, *Rectifiability: A Survey*** (2023, Cambridge
  University Press, London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series).
  Listed in full in Section 37. Densities, tangents, and the fine
  structure of measures.
* **Stanislav Hencl and Pekka Koskela, *Lectures on Mappings of Finite
  Distortion*** (2014, Springer, Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Listed
  in full in Section 44. Differentiability and distortion in the
  Sobolev category.

## 6. Probability: Measure-Theoretic Foundations and Limit Theory

Durrett, Kallenberg, and Billingsley remain the standards. The recent
books are new graduate courses and the limit theory of processes on
graphs, trees, and groups.

* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Measure Theory, Probability, and Stochastic
  Processes*** (2022, Springer, Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A
  graduate course from measure theory to martingales, Markov chains,
  and Brownian motion, with the analytic connections kept in view.
* **Erhan Çinlar, *Probability and Stochastics*** (2011, Springer,
  Graduate Texts in Mathematics). A broad graduate text in the
  Strasbourg tradition, strong on conditioning and processes.
* **Allan Gut, *Probability: A Graduate Course*** (2nd ed., 2013,
  Springer). A systematic course in limit theorems, characteristic
  functions, and convergence.
* **Daniel W. Stroock, *Mathematics of Probability*** (2013, American
  Mathematical Society, Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A short
  analyst's course, distinct from Stroock's *Probability Theory: An
  Analytic View*.
* **Firas Rassoul-Agha and Timo Seppäläinen, *A Course on Large
  Deviations with an Introduction to Gibbs Measures*** (2015, American
  Mathematical Society, Graduate Studies in Mathematics). A modern
  graduate route into large deviations, closer to statistical mechanics
  than Dembo–Zeitouni.
* **S. R. S. Varadhan, *Large Deviations*** (2016, American Mathematical
  Society, Courant Lecture Notes). Varadhan's own short account of the
  theory he founded.
* **Andreas E. Kyprianou, *Fluctuations of Lévy Processes with
  Applications*** (2nd ed., 2014, Springer). Path decomposition, exit
  problems, and the fluctuation theory that Sato's treatise does not
  emphasise.
* **David Applebaum, *Lévy Processes and Stochastic Calculus*** (2nd ed.,
  2009, Cambridge University Press). Stochastic integration for
  processes with jumps, the usual companion to the pathwise theory.
* **Gregory F. Lawler and Vlada Limic, *Random Walk: A Modern
  Introduction*** (2010, Cambridge University Press). Potential theory,
  intersection, and the heat kernel for random walk, written for
  analysts.
* **Russell Lyons and Yuval Peres, *Probability on Trees and Networks***
  (2016, Cambridge University Press). Branching processes, percolation,
  uniform spanning trees, and random walk on graphs, in one systematic
  book.
* **Geoffrey Grimmett, *Probability on Graphs: Random Processes on
  Graphs and Lattices*** (2nd ed., 2018, Cambridge University Press). A
  short graduate course on percolation, random-cluster models, and
  related processes.
* **Remco van der Hofstad, *Random Graphs and Complex Networks***,
  volume I (2017) and volume II (2024, Cambridge University Press). The
  current graduate reference for random graphs, from local weak
  convergence to mesoscopic structure.
* **Olav Kallenberg, *Random Measures, Theory and Applications*** (2017,
  Springer). The research treatise on random measures and point
  processes, separate from Kallenberg's *Foundations*.

## 7. Concentration, High-Dimensional Probability, and Empirical Processes

Vershynin, Boucheron–Lugosi–Massart, and Talagrand are already in the
classics list. The books here are the statistical companions, the random
matrix monographs, and the refined concentration theory.

* **Martin J. Wainwright, *High-Dimensional Statistics: A Non-Asymptotic
  Viewpoint*** (2019, Cambridge University Press). Nonasymptotic
  empirical processes, sparsity, and random matrices, written as a
  statistics course that analysts can read.
* **Evarist Giné and Richard Nickl, *Mathematical Foundations of
  Infinite-Dimensional Statistical Models*** (2016, Cambridge University
  Press). Empirical processes, concentration, and nonparametric theory
  in the form closest to classical analysis.
* **Vladimir Koltchinskii, *Oracle Inequalities in Empirical Risk
  Minimization and Sparse Recovery Problems*** (2011, Springer, Lecture
  Notes in Mathematics). A research monograph on empirical risk and
  sparsity, in the Saint-Flour tradition.
* **Sara van de Geer, *Estimation and Testing Under Sparsity*** (2016,
  Springer, Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Oracle inequalities and
  high-dimensional testing, again from the empirical-process side.
* **Sourav Chatterjee, *Superconcentration and Related Topics*** (2014,
  Springer). What happens when classical concentration is sharper than
  the usual variance bound, with examples from spin glasses and
  permutations.
* **Joel A. Tropp, *An Introduction to Matrix Concentration
  Inequalities*** (2015, Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning).
  The short systematic account of matrix Bernstein and Chernoff bounds.
* **Elizabeth S. Meckes, *The Random Matrix Theory of the Classical
  Compact Groups*** (2019, Cambridge University Press). Eigenvalue
  distribution, concentration, and the classical groups, at graduate
  level.
* **Peter J. Forrester, *Log-Gases and Random Matrices*** (2010,
  Princeton University Press, London Mathematical Society). The large
  reference for exact random-matrix computations and log-gases.
* **László Erdős and Horng-Tzer Yau, *A Dynamical Approach to Random
  Matrix Theory*** (2017, American Mathematical Society, Courant Lecture
  Notes). Local semicircle laws and universality through Dyson Brownian
  motion.
* **Jinho Baik, Percy Deift, and Toufic Suidan, *Combinatorics and
  Random Matrix Theory*** (2016, American Mathematical Society, Graduate
  Studies in Mathematics). Longest increasing subsequences, random
  matrices, and the Riemann–Hilbert method in combinatorial form.
* **Guillaume Aubrun and Stanisław J. Szarek, *Alice and Bob Meet
  Banach: The Interface of Asymptotic Geometric Analysis and Quantum
  Information Theory*** (2017, American Mathematical Society). Listed in
  full in Section 13.

## 8. Stochastic Processes, Martingales, and Stochastic Calculus

Karatzas–Shreve, Revuz–Yor, and Rogers–Williams remain the references.
The recent books are new courses, jump processes, random geometry, and
mean-field games.

* **Jean-François Le Gall, *Brownian Motion, Martingales, and Stochastic
  Calculus*** (2016, Springer, Graduate Texts in Mathematics). The
  current standard French-school graduate course, shorter than
  Karatzas–Shreve and strong on sample-path properties.
* **René L. Schilling, Lothar Partzsch, and Björn Böttcher, *Brownian
  Motion: An Introduction to Stochastic Processes*** (2nd ed., 2014,
  De Gruyter). A detailed first course with complete proofs and a large
  exercise set.
* **Paolo Baldi, *Stochastic Calculus: An Introduction Through Theory
  and Exercises*** (2017, Springer). A graduate stochastic-calculus
  course organised around exercises.
* **Fabrice Baudoin, *Diffusion Processes and Stochastic Calculus***
  (2014, European Mathematical Society). Diffusions from the analytic
  side, including rough-path and geometric remarks.
* **Hiroshi Kunita, *Stochastic Flows and Jump-Diffusions*** (2019,
  Springer). A new treatise on stochastic flows, distinct from Kunita's
  1990 monograph, with jumps included.
* **Yves Le Jan, *Markov Paths, Loops and Fields*** (2011, Springer,
  Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Loop measures, the Gaussian free field,
  and the isomorphism theorems, from the Saint-Flour lectures.
* **Alain-Sol Sznitman, *Topics in Occupation Times and Gaussian Free
  Fields*** (2012, European Mathematical Society, Zurich Lectures in
  Advanced Mathematics). Isomorphism theorems and cover times, in a
  short research course.
* **Christophe Garban and Jeffrey E. Steif, *Noise Sensitivity of
  Boolean Functions and Percolation*** (2014, Cambridge University
  Press). Noise sensitivity, hypercontractivity, and the Fourier
  analysis of percolation.
* **Tadahisa Funaki, *Lectures on Random Interfaces*** (2016, Springer).
  Stochastic PDE models of interfaces, including the KPZ equation in an
  analytic setting.
* **Nathanaël Berestycki and Ellen Powell, *Gaussian Free Field and
  Liouville Quantum Gravity*** (2025, Cambridge University Press,
  Cambridge Studies in Advanced Mathematics). A systematic introduction
  to the Gaussian free field and the random geometry built from it.
* **David Applebaum, *Probability on Compact Lie Groups*** (2014,
  Springer). Probability, convolution, and analysis on compact groups.
* **Nizar Touzi, *Optimal Stochastic Control, Stochastic Target
  Problems, and Backward SDE*** (2013, Springer). A research course on
  backward equations and stochastic control.
* **Jianfeng Zhang, *Backward Stochastic Differential Equations: From
  Linear to Fully Nonlinear Theory*** (2017, Springer). The analytic
  theory of BSDEs, through fully nonlinear equations.
* **René Carmona and François Delarue, *Probabilistic Theory of Mean
  Field Games with Applications*** (2 volumes, 2018, Springer). The
  large probabilistic treatment of mean-field games.
* **Pierre Cardaliaguet, François Delarue, Jean-Michel Lasry, and
  Pierre-Louis Lions, *The Master Equation and the Convergence Problem
  in Mean Field Games*** (2019, Princeton University Press, Annals of
  Mathematics Studies). The master equation and the passage from finite
  to infinite player games.
* **Alain Bensoussan, Jens Frehse, and Phillip Yam, *Mean Field Games
  and Mean Field Type Control Theory*** (2013, Springer). The
  PDE-and-control route into the same subject.
* **Amarjit Budhiraja and Paul Dupuis, *Analysis and Approximation of
  Rare Events: Representations and Weak Convergence Methods for
  Infinitely Divisible Processes*** (2019, Springer). Large deviations
  for jump processes by weak-convergence methods.

## 9. Malliavin Calculus, Rough Paths, and Stochastic PDE

Friz–Hairer, Friz–Victoir, Nualart, and Da Prato–Zabczyk are already in
the classics list. The books here are textbooks and the variational and
random-field approaches to stochastic PDE.

* **David Nualart and Eulalia Nualart, *Introduction to Malliavin
  Calculus*** (2018, Cambridge University Press). A graduate textbook,
  shorter and more introductory than Nualart's research monograph.
* **Giuseppe Da Prato, *Introduction to Stochastic Analysis and
  Malliavin Calculus*** (2014, Edizioni della Normale). Gaussian
  analysis on Hilbert space and the Malliavin calculus, in a short
  course.
* **Vlad Bally and Lucia Caramellino, *Stochastic Integration by Parts
  and Functional Itô Calculus*** (2016, Birkhäuser). Integration by
  parts for jump processes, and the functional Itô calculus.
* **Wei Liu and Michael Röckner, *Stochastic Partial Differential
  Equations: An Introduction*** (2015, Springer). The variational
  approach to SPDE, as a textbook continuing Prévôt–Röckner.
* **Davar Khoshnevisan, *Analysis of Stochastic Partial Differential
  Equations*** (2014, American Mathematical Society, CBMS Regional
  Conference Series). The random-field approach: intermittency, peaks,
  and the parabolic Anderson model.
* **Robert C. Dalang, Davar Khoshnevisan, Carl Mueller, David Nualart,
  and Yimin Xiao, *A Minicourse on Stochastic Partial Differential
  Equations*** (2009, Springer, Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Six
  short courses that still orient the subject.
* **Feng-Yu Wang, *Harnack Inequalities for Stochastic Partial
  Differential Equations*** (2013, Springer). Coupling and Harnack
  inequalities for stochastic evolution equations.
* **Lorenzo Zambotti, *Random Obstacle Problems*** (2017, Springer,
  Lecture Notes in Mathematics). Reflected SPDEs and the contact set,
  from the Saint-Flour lectures.
* **Tadahisa Funaki, *Lectures on Random Interfaces*** (2016, Springer).
  Listed in Section 8. Interface models as singular SPDEs.
* **Sergei Kuksin and Armen Shirikyan, *Mathematics of Two-Dimensional
  Turbulence*** (2012, Cambridge University Press). Listed in Section
  34. The stochastic Navier–Stokes equation as a dynamical system.

## 10. Ergodic Theory and Measurable Dynamics

Walters, Einsiedler–Ward, Host–Kra, and Katok–Hasselblatt are already
in the classics list. The recent books fill in smooth ergodic theory,
homogeneous random walks, and several textbook routes.

* **David Kerr and Hanfeng Li, *Ergodic Theory: Independence and
  Dichotomies*** (2016, Springer). Entropy, sofic and combinatorial
  independence, and the measurable structure of group actions.
* **Marcelo Viana and Krerley Oliveira, *Foundations of Ergodic Theory***
  (2016, Cambridge University Press). A modern graduate course from
  invariant measures to entropy and the ergodic theorem.
* **Yves Coudène, *Ergodic Theory and Dynamical Systems*** (2016,
  Springer). A short course bridging topological and measurable
  dynamics.
* **Marcelo Viana, *Lectures on Lyapunov Exponents*** (2014, Cambridge
  University Press). The multiplicative ergodic theorem and its
  consequences, in a concise graduate account.
* **Luis Barreira, *Lyapunov Exponents*** (2017, Birkhäuser). A more
  expansive treatment of the same circle, including dimension.
* **Todd Fisher and Boris Hasselblatt, *Hyperbolic Flows*** (2019,
  European Mathematical Society, Zurich Lectures in Advanced
  Mathematics). Continuous-time hyperbolic dynamics, shadowing, and
  structural stability.
* **José F. Alves, *Nonuniformly Hyperbolic Attractors: Geometric and
  Probabilistic Aspects*** (2020, Springer). Statistical properties of
  attractors with singularities, in the Viana school.
* **Christian Bonatti, Lorenzo J. Díaz, and Marcelo Viana, *Dynamics
  Beyond Uniform Hyperbolicity: A Global Geometric and Probabilistic
  Perspective*** (2005, Springer). The research monograph on partial
  hyperbolicity and robust transitivity.
* **Yves Benoist and Jean-François Quint, *Random Walks on Reductive
  Groups*** (2016, Springer, Ergebnisse der Mathematik). Random walks
  on homogeneous spaces, stationary measures, and stiffness.
* **Dave Witte Morris, *Ratner's Theorems on Unipotent Flows*** (2005,
  University of Chicago Press). The readable account of Ratner theory.
* **Robert J. Zimmer and Dave Witte Morris, *Ergodic Theory, Groups,
  and Geometry*** (2008, American Mathematical Society, CBMS). Cocycle
  superrigidity in a short conference monograph.
* **Anatole Katok and Viorel Niţică, *Rigidity in Higher Rank Abelian
  Group Actions***, volume I (2011, Cambridge University Press). KAM
  and measure rigidity for higher-rank abelian actions.
* **Terence Tao, *Higher Order Fourier Analysis*** (2012, American
  Mathematical Society). Listed in full in Section 27. Gowers norms,
  nilsequences, and the ergodic-theoretic form of higher-order Fourier
  analysis.
* **Alexander S. Kechris, *Global Aspects of Ergodic Group Actions***
  (2010, American Mathematical Society). Listed in Section 3.

# Part III. Inequalities, Convexity, and Order

---

## 11. Classical Inequalities, Means, Majorization, and Rearrangement

Hardy–Littlewood–Pólya, Marshall–Olkin–Arnold, and Lieb–Loss remain the
references. The recent books treat Hardy inequalities, symmetrization,
and the stability of matter.

* **Alexander A. Balinsky, W. Desmond Evans, and Roger T. Lewis, *The
  Analysis and Geometry of Hardy's Inequality*** (2015, Springer).
  Hardy inequalities on domains and manifolds, with the geometric
  conditions made explicit.
* **Alois Kufner, Lars-Erik Persson, and Natasha Samko, *Weighted
  Inequalities of Hardy Type*** (2nd ed., 2017, World Scientific). The
  updated reference for weighted Hardy inequalities and their
  constants.
* **Michael Ruzhansky and Durvudkhan Suragan, *Hardy Inequalities on
  Homogeneous Groups*** (2019, Birkhäuser). Hardy inequalities in the
  subelliptic and homogeneous-group setting.
* **Elliott H. Lieb and Robert Seiringer, *The Stability of Matter in
  Quantum Mechanics*** (2010, Cambridge University Press). Lieb–Thirring
  inequalities, electrostatic inequalities, and the analytic proof of
  stability of matter.
* **Albert Baernstein II, *Symmetrization in Analysis***, with David
  Drasin and Richard S. Laugesen (2019, Cambridge University Press).
  Symmetrization, polarization, and the \*-function, completed after
  Baernstein's death. The modern monograph on the method.

---

## 12. Convexity, Convex Analysis, and Variational Analysis

Rockafellar, Rockafellar–Wets, and Bauschke–Combettes are already in the
classics list. The recent books extend the calculus to Banach spaces and
record the first-order methods that now sit next to it.

* **Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*** (2004,
  Cambridge University Press). The standard course in finite-dimensional
  convex optimisation. Earlier than the nominal boundary, and still the
  book analysts open for conic formulations and duality in applications.
* **Yurii Nesterov, *Lectures on Convex Optimization*** (2nd ed., 2018,
  Springer). Complexity of first-order methods, self-concordance, and
  the structure of smooth convex problems.
* **Amir Beck, *First-Order Methods in Optimization*** (2017, SIAM).
  Proximal methods, acceleration, and the convex-analytic facts those
  algorithms use.
* **Jean-Paul Penot, *Calculus Without Derivatives*** (2013, Springer,

# How to use this list beside the classics bibliography

Read `CKMA-bib.md` for the books that organised a subject, and read this
file for a later textbook or a later research synthesis. In several
sections the later synthesis is already in the classics list, and this
file is then short. That is deliberate. Examples are rough paths, the
vector-valued Calderón–Zygmund theory, optimal transport on metric
measure spaces, and the decoupling theorem: the books that play that
role are already listed there.

A book in this file is not a claim that it has replaced a classic. It is
a claim that a graduate student or a specialist can usefully open it
now, and that knowing it exists is worth more than searching a
publisher's catalogue from scratch.

The same boundaries apply as in the classics list. Numerical analysis,
statistics, and analytic number theory appear only where the book is
also a book of analysis. Areas whose literature is still a sequence of
papers are represented by the nearest stable monograph, or omitted.
