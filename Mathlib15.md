## Quotients and the bidual

The quotient by a subspace carries the quotient seminorm \(\|x+M\|=\inf_{m\in M}\|x+m\|\). When the subspace is closed this is a norm, compatible with the quotient topology. The canonical projection is continuous with norm at most one, and continuous linear maps vanishing on the subspace descend to the quotient. A quotient of a Banach space by a closed subspace is complete (`Analysis/Normed/Group/Quotient.lean`, `Submodule.Quotient.completeSpace`).

For a real or complex normed space the canonical map \(J:E\to E^{**}\), defined by \(J(x)(\ell)=\ell(x)\), is a linear isometry. With the weak topology on \(E\) and the weak-star topology on \(E^{**}\), it is a topological embedding. A bounded set \(S\subset E\) has compact weak closure if the weak-star closure of \(J(S)\) is contained in \(J(E)\) (`Analysis/Normed/Module/DoubleDual.lean`, `inclusionInDoubleDualLi`, `isCompact_closure_of_isBounded`). In particular, surjectivity of \(J\) supplies the usual weak compactness conclusion for bounded weakly closed sets.

## Open mapping and the closed graph

The maps in the open-mapping file are continuous semilinear maps between normed spaces over nontrivially normed fields, and the scalar homomorphisms are inverse to each other and isometric (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Banach.lean`).

Completeness is introduced in two steps. While only the codomain is complete, Baire’s theorem gives an approximate preimage: a surjective continuous semilinear map admits a constant \(C \ge 0\) such that every \(y\) is within distance \(\|y\|/2\) of the image of some \(x\) with \(\|x\| \le C\|y\|\). Once the domain is complete as well, the iterative series of these approximations converges, and there is a constant \(C > 0\) such that every \(y\) has an exact preimage \(x\) with \(\|x\| \le C\|y\|\). The same pair of completeness assumptions makes a surjective continuous semilinear map an open map, and a quotient map. A surjective map admits a right inverse, possibly nonlinear, with \(\|x\| \le C\|y\|\). The file records that a continuous linear right inverse need not exist: the quotient by a closed subspace with no closed complement is the example given there.

A continuous surjective affine map between normed affine spaces modelled on Banach spaces is an open map. The domain model is already complete when this statement is reached, and completeness of the codomain model is assumed again in the statement.

A bijective continuous semilinear map between Banach spaces, still with inverse isometric scalar homomorphisms, upgrades to a continuous semilinear equivalence. Both spaces are complete at that declaration. The comment attached to the declaration mentions a Banach space and a normed space; the variables in scope include completeness of the codomain, and the argument uses the open-mapping step. A continuous linear endomorphism of a Banach space is a unit if and only if it is bijective, and its spectrum coincides with its spectrum as an algebraic endomorphism.

For continuous linear maps between Banach spaces over the same scalar field, an injective map has closed range if and only if it is antilipschitz, and then it has a continuous left inverse defined on the range. If the range is algebraically complemented by a closed submodule and the kernel is trivial, the range is closed. The file marks removal of the trivial-kernel hypothesis as future work. Its accompanying comment about quotient normed spaces is outdated: those spaces are already constructed in `Analysis/Normed/Group/Quotient.lean`. An antilipschitz continuous semilinear map on a Banach domain has closed range, with no completeness hypothesis on the codomain at that line. If both spaces are Banach spaces and the scalar homomorphisms are inverse and isometric, bijectivity is equivalent to dense range together with an antilipschitz bound.

The closed graph theorem is for linear maps between two Banach spaces over one common scalar field, not for semilinear maps. A linear map with closed graph is continuous. Sequentially: if \(u_n \to x\) and \(g(u_n) \to y\) imply \(y = g(x)\), then \(g\) is continuous. Either hypothesis upgrades the linear map to a continuous linear map (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Banach.lean`).

A different open-mapping theorem concerns group homomorphisms. A continuous surjective homomorphism from a σ-compact topological group to a Hausdorff topological group which is a Baire space is an open map, and the orbit map of a continuous transitive action of a σ-compact group on a Hausdorff Baire space is open. The file records that σ-compactness is essential: the identity from the discrete line to the usual line is continuous and bijective, and both groups are locally compact, but the map is not open (`Topology/Algebra/Group/OpenMapping.lean`).

## Hahn–Banach

On a seminormed space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), a continuous linear functional on a subspace extends to the whole space with the same operator norm. The space need not be complete. If \(x\) is nonzero, some continuous linear functional of norm \(1\) takes the value \(\|x\|\) at \(x\), with the norm read in the scalar field. For an arbitrary vector the same conclusion holds with norm at most \(1\). If the space is a nontrivial normed space, a functional of norm exactly \(1\) exists for every vector, the zero vector included (`Analysis/Normed/Module/HahnBanach.lean`).

The geometric forms are proved for a real topological vector space, before local convexity is assumed. Disjoint convex sets, one of them open, are weakly separated by a continuous real-linear functional. If both are open, the separation is semistrict: the two images lie on opposite sides of a common value, both inequalities strict. If one convex set has nonempty interior and is disjoint from another convex set, the separation is nonstrict, and the functional may be taken nonzero when the second set is nonempty. For a convex set \(A\) with nonempty interior and \(x\notin\operatorname{int}A\), a nonzero continuous linear functional satisfies \(f(y)\le f(x)\) for all \(y\in A\). When \(x\in A\), this is an attained maximum; a point outside \(A\) need not be a maximizer on \(A\) (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Separation.lean`).

Local convexity is the hypothesis for strict separation. In a real locally convex space, a compact convex set and a disjoint closed convex set are strictly separated, with a gap between the two values. A point outside a closed convex set is strictly separated from it. In a \(T_1\) space, distinct points are strictly separated, so the dual separates points. A closed convex set is the intersection of the closed half-spaces containing it. The same statements are repeated for a space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), with continuous linear functionals over that field and with real parts in the inequalities. A real or complex normed space is locally convex, so these forms apply to it (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Separation.lean`).

The analytic theorem for polynormable spaces, which Section 16 develops, extends a continuous linear functional on a subspace without a norm-preservation claim in the bare extension statement, and extends a functional dominated by a continuous seminorm with the same domination. A continuous linear map from a subspace into a Hausdorff topological vector space, with finite-dimensional range, extends, and a finite-dimensional submodule of a polynormable space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) is topologically complemented (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/HahnBanach.lean`).

## Uniform boundedness

A family of continuous semilinear maps from a complete seminormed space into a seminormed space, over nontrivially normed fields and along an isometric scalar homomorphism, is uniformly bounded in operator norm as soon as it is pointwise bounded. The file describes this as the theorem for maps from a Banach space into a normed space, and deduces it from the barrelled form. The same bound is stated with suprema in the extended nonnegative reals (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/BanachSteinhaus.lean`).

A space is barrelled when every lower-semicontinuous seminorm is continuous. The definition is stated for an arbitrary nontrivially normed scalar field. A Baire topological vector space over such a field is barrelled, and the file records that this includes Banach spaces and Fréchet spaces. A pointwise seminorm-bounded family of continuous semilinear maps from a barrelled space into a space with a seminorm topology is uniformly equicontinuous. If the codomain is polynormable, von Neumann boundedness of each orbit gives the same conclusion. A pointwise limit of continuous semilinear maps from a barrelled space into a Hausdorff polynormable space, along a countably generated nontrivial filter, is continuous; the argument reduces the filter to a sequence (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Barrelled.lean`). Section 16 records the barrelled theory at more length.

## Closed range, again, and Alaoglu

The closed-range facts proved with the open-mapping theorem are the antilipschitz characterization and the complemented-range statement of the first section. On a Hilbert space the kernel of an adjoint and the closure of the range of the adjoint are orthogonal complements of each other; that identity is stated with the adjoint below. A theorem identifying closed range of a general operator with closed range of its adjoint was not found.

Banach–Alaoglu has two forms. In the operator space, if the codomain is a proper metric space, the set of continuous semilinear maps at distance at most \(r\) from a given map is compact in the topology of pointwise convergence (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Completeness.lean`). In the dual of a normed space, not necessarily complete, closed balls are compact in the weak-star topology when the scalar field is proper, and so is the polar of a neighbourhood of the origin. The scalar fields \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbb{C}\) are proper. If the normed space is separable, those balls and polars are sequentially compact, and every weak-star compact subset of the dual is metrizable. On the weak dual of a Banach space, norm-boundedness and von Neumann boundedness coincide, by the uniform boundedness principle (`Analysis/Normed/Module/WeakDual.lean`).

## Riesz's lemma and local compactness

Riesz's lemma: for a closed proper subspace \(F\) of a normed space and any \(r<1\), some nonzero \(x\) satisfies \(\operatorname{dist}(x,F)\ge r\|x\|\); over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) one may take \(\|x\|=1\) (`Analysis/Normed/Module/RieszLemma.lean`). A normed space over a complete nontrivially normed field that is locally compact is finite-dimensional, and a finite-dimensional normed space over a complete field is complete, with all norms equivalent (`Analysis/Normed/Module/FiniteDimension.lean`, `Topology/Algebra/Module/FiniteDimension.lean`).

## Schauder bases

A generalized Schauder basis of a normed space, over a nontrivially normed field, is a family of vectors, a family of coordinate functionals in the continuous dual, a biorthogonality relation, and an expansion of every vector as a sum along a specified summation filter. The classical Schauder basis is the case indexed by \(\mathbb{N}\), with conditional sums of partial sums. An unconditional Schauder basis is indexed by an arbitrary type, with sums over finite subsets. The vectors are linearly independent. The projection onto a finite set of basis vectors has range equal to their span and rank equal to the cardinality of the set. These projections converge pointwise to the identity, and the projection onto \(A\) composed with the projection onto \(B\) is the projection onto the intersection. For a classical basis, the \(n\)th and \(m\)th projections compose as the projection of index \(\min(n,m)\) (`Analysis/Normed/Module/Bases.lean`).

If the space is complete, the partial-sum projections of a classical Schauder basis are uniformly bounded, by Banach–Steinhaus, and so are all finite projections of an unconditional basis.

A classical basis can be built from nested finite-rank projections. The data are a sequence of continuous endomorphisms with \(P_0 = 0\), with the range of \(P_n\) of dimension \(n\), with \(P_n \circ P_m = P_{\min(n,m)}\), and with pointwise convergence to the identity, together with a nonzero vector in the range of each rank-one difference \(P_{n+1} - P_n\). These vectors and the coefficient functionals recovered from the differences form a classical Schauder basis whose projections are the original maps (`Analysis/Normed/Module/Bases.lean`). A Hilbert basis, below, is an unconditional Schauder basis, and a Hilbert basis indexed by \(\mathbb{N}\) is a classical Schauder basis.

## Compact operators and the Fredholm alternative

A map from a pointed topological space is a compact operator when the preimage of some compact set is a neighbourhood of zero. If the codomain is Hausdorff, this is equivalent to the closure of the image of some neighbourhood of zero being compact. For a continuous action of the scalars, images of balls have compact closure in a Hausdorff codomain. The zero map is compact. The identity is compact if and only if the additive topological group is locally compact. Compactness passes to pre- and post-composition with continuous linear maps, and to continuous post-composition in general. A continuous linear map into, or out of, a locally compact additive topological group is compact. The restriction of a compact operator to a closed invariant submodule is compact. From a seminormed space into a complete Hausdorff uniform topological vector space, the compact semilinear operators form a closed set, so a limit of compact operators is compact (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic.lean`).

On a Hausdorff topological vector space over a complete nontrivially normed field, compactness of the identity implies finite dimensionality. The comment on that lemma says “Banach space”; the variables require no norm and no completeness of the space. If the scalar field is itself locally compact, the identity is compact if and only if the space is finite-dimensional (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/FiniteDimension.lean`).

The Fredholm alternative is stated for a compact endomorphism of a normed space over an arbitrary nontrivially normed field. The argument uses shells rather than an exact unit sphere, because a general normed field need not rescale a vector to norm exactly \(1\). If \(\mu \ne 0\) is not an eigenvalue, then \(T - \mu \mathrm{id}\) is antilipschitz. Completeness of the space is not assumed for this lower bound. If the space is complete and \(\mu \ne 0\), then either \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue or \(\mu\) lies in the resolvent, and the nonzero spectrum consists exactly of the nonzero eigenvalues. The resolvent step uses the criterion that a continuous endomorphism of a Banach space is invertible if and only if it is bijective (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/FredholmAlternative.lean`).

## Fredholm operators

Fredholm operators are continuous linear maps between modules over a nontrivially normed field, carrying topologies. They need not be normed or complete. The definition asks that the map be strict, that the range be closed and of finite codimension, and that the kernel be finite-dimensional and topologically complemented. The definition records that a later theorem, not in the library, should deduce all of this from finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel for maps between Banach or Fréchet spaces over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Fredholm/Basic.lean`).

When the scalar field is complete and both spaces are Hausdorff topological vector spaces, four conditions are equivalent: the definition; existence of a continuous quasi-inverse; an isomorphism, induced by the operator, between closed finite-codimension subspaces; and a Fredholm package, meaning topological decompositions of the domain and codomain with finite-dimensional complements such that the operator is an equivalence on the essential parts and zero on the finite-dimensional part. Under those scalar and codomain hypotheses, the range of a Fredholm operator is topologically complemented. The index is the difference of the dimension of the kernel and the dimension of the cokernel. The algebraic definition returns an integer in any case, and is meaningful once both dimensions are finite, as they are for a Fredholm operator. The index of a composition of Fredholm operators is the sum of the indices (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Fredholm/Basic.lean`, `Algebra/Module/LinearMap/Index.lean`).

Openness of the Fredholm operators is proved for continuous linear maps from a Banach space into a normed space, over a complete nontrivially normed field. The comments in the file say “two Banach spaces”. Completeness of the codomain is not among the variables. The argument completes only the essential subspace of the domain and then uses openness of invertibility, which itself is stated for continuous linear maps out of a Banach space into a seminormed space (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/BoundedLinearMaps.lean`). The Fredholm operators are open in the operator-norm topology, and the index is locally constant on that set. An analytic choice of quasi-inverse near a given Fredholm operator is marked as not yet proved (`Analysis/Normed/Operator/Fredholm/Open.lean`).

## \(\ell^p\)

For a family of normed groups, \(\ell^p\) is the subgroup of the product consisting of families with finitely many nonzero terms when \(p = 0\), with summable \(p\)th powers of the norms when \(0 < p < \infty\), and with bounded norms when \(p = \infty\). For \(1 \le p \le \infty\) this is a normed additive group, and a normed space when each factor is a normed space over a normed field. If \(1 \le p\) and each factor is complete, \(\ell^p\) is complete. For \(1 \le p < \infty\), the finitely supported families are dense: the canonical single-coordinate series sums to the family. Membership at an exponent \(q\) implies membership at every larger exponent, so \(\ell^p \subset \ell^q\) whenever \(p \le q\). A pointwise limit of a norm-bounded family in \(\ell^p\) remains in \(\ell^p\) (`Analysis/Normed/Lp/lpSpace.lean`).

Scalar Hölder: if the real parts of \(p\) and \(q\) are conjugate, then \(\sum_i \|f_i\|\,\|g_i\| \le \|f\|\,\|g\|\). The file marks the endpoint \(p = 1\), \(q = \infty\) as not covered by that formulation. A uniformly bounded family of continuous bilinear maps, over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), induces a map \(\ell^p \times \ell^q \to \ell^r\) for a Hölder triple, including the extreme exponents, and the map is continuous of operator norm at most the uniform bound when \(1 \le p,q,r\). For conjugate exponents and a complete target, the same data give a continuous pairing by summation. A uniformly bounded family of continuous linear maps induces a continuous linear map on \(\ell^p\) for \(1 \le p\) (`Analysis/Normed/Lp/lpHolder.lean`).

Completeness of the measure-theoretic spaces \(L^p\), Hölder’s inequality there, and density of simple functions are recorded in `Mathlib04.md`. The pairing of \(L^p\) with \(L^q\) is constructed there; the identification of the dual of \(L^p\) with \(L^q\) is not.

## Hilbert space

Throughout this section the scalar field is \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\).

An inner product induces a conjugate-linear isometric embedding of a seminormed inner product space into its continuous dual, by \(x \mapsto (y \mapsto \langle x, y \rangle)\). If the space is complete, this embedding is a conjugate-linear isometric equivalence onto the continuous dual. The dual in this statement is the continuous dual (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Dual.lean`).

In a real inner product space, a nonempty complete convex set has, for every point, a point of the set at minimal distance. The comment calls that point unique; the theorem that is stated is existence. The minimizer is characterized, without completeness, by the variational inequality \(\langle u - v, w - v \rangle_{\mathbb{R}} \le 0\) for every \(w\) in the set. For a complete subspace of a real or complex inner product space, a distance minimizer exists, and it is characterized by orthogonality of the residual to the subspace. A point of a subspace with that orthogonality property is the orthogonal projection, and it is the only such point. Complete subspaces have an orthogonal projection. The projection onto the subspace and the projection viewed as an endomorphism of the ambient space are continuous and linear. When the projection exists, the space is the topological direct sum of the subspace and its orthogonal. The adjoint of the inclusion of a complete subspace is the orthogonal projection onto that subspace (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Minimal.lean`, `Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Basic.lean`).

The orthogonal of a submodule is closed, meets the submodule only at zero, and contains the submodule in its double orthogonal. The triple orthogonal equals the orthogonal. In a Hilbert space the double orthogonal equals the closure. The file records that completeness is essential for that equality. A closed submodule which admits an orthogonal projection equals its double orthogonal. In a Hilbert space, a submodule is dense if and only if its orthogonal is zero (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Orthogonal.lean`, `Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Projection/Submodule.lean`).

An orthonormal family has norm-one vectors and vanishing inner products off the diagonal, and satisfies Bessel’s inequality for finite sums and for the series of squared moduli of the coefficients. An orthonormal set extends to a maximal orthonormal set (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Orthonormal.lean`).

Gram–Schmidt, indexed by a well-ordered type, subtracts the projections onto the lines already produced. It preserves span. On a linearly independent family the normalized output is orthonormal, and a basis is sent to a basis. Dropping the vectors that become zero produces an orthonormal family without a linear-independence hypothesis. Ambient completeness is not required (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/GramSchmidtOrtho.lean`).

The adjoint is defined for continuous linear maps between Hilbert spaces, so both the domain and the codomain are complete. It is a conjugate-linear isometric equivalence from the operators \(E \to F\) onto the operators \(F \to E\), characterized by \(\langle A^\dagger y, x \rangle = \langle y, A x \rangle\), involutive, reversing composition, and preserving the operator norm. The orthogonal of the range is the kernel of the adjoint. The orthogonal of the kernel is the closure of the range of the adjoint. A vector in the domain minimizes the distance from a given vector to the range if and only if the adjoint sends the residual to zero. A continuous endomorphism is self-adjoint if and only if it equals its adjoint, if and only if it is symmetric. On a Hilbert space, an everywhere-defined symmetric linear operator is continuous; this is the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem, proved from the sequential closed graph theorem (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Adjoint.lean`, `Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Symmetric.lean`).

In finite dimensions the algebraic adjoint satisfies the same inner-product identity, and the orthogonal of the kernel equals the range of the adjoint exactly, because a finite-dimensional range is closed (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Adjoint.lean`).

The star operation on continuous endomorphisms of a Hilbert space is the adjoint, and \(\|A^\dagger A\| = \|A\|^2\). The \(C^\star\)-algebra structure in the complex case is recorded with Section 23.

The \(\ell^2\) sum of a family of inner product spaces is an inner product space, with inner product the sum of the coordinate inner products. It is complete when each factor is, by the completeness theorem for \(\ell^p\). A Hilbert basis is a linear isometric equivalence with \(\ell^2\). Its coordinate functionals are the inner products against the basis vectors, the expansion \(x = \sum_i \langle b_i, x \rangle b_i\) converges unconditionally, and
\[
\sum_i \langle x, b_i \rangle \langle b_i, y \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle.
\]
Finite linear combinations of the basis vectors are dense. On a Hilbert space, an orthonormal family whose span is dense, or whose span has trivial orthogonal, is a Hilbert basis. Every Hilbert space has a Hilbert basis, extending any prescribed orthonormal set. A complete subspace and its orthogonal form a Hilbert sum (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/l2Space.lean`).

The separation quotient and the uniform completion of an inner product space are inner product spaces, and the inner product extends the original one (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Completion.lean`).

The Lax–Milgram theorem: a bounded coercive bilinear form on a real Hilbert space is represented by a continuous linear equivalence of the space with itself, so that every continuous linear functional is \(v\mapsto B(u,v)\) for a unique \(u\) (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/LaxMilgram.lean`). Its use for weak solutions of PDE is recorded in Sections 32 and 33.

A reproducing kernel Hilbert space is a Hilbert space of functions on which point evaluations are continuous. Its kernel is positive semidefinite, the kernel functions are dense, and conversely every positive semidefinite kernel determines such a space (`Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Reproducing.lean`); see also Section 42.

The von Neumann mean ergodic theorem, for a continuous linear contraction on a Hilbert space, is stated in Section 10.

## Arzelà–Ascoli

The compactness theory of function spaces includes Arzelà–Ascoli. On a compact domain, an equicontinuous family of continuous maps into a metric space whose values at each point lie in a compact set has compact closure in the uniform topology. There is also a general version for uniform convergence on families of compact subsets (`Topology/UniformSpace/Ascoli.lean`) and a bounded-continuous-function version (`Topology/ContinuousMap/Bounded/ArzelaAscoli.lean`). On an equicontinuous family over a compact domain, pointwise and uniform convergence induce the same topology.

As a consequence, finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel suffice for Fredholmness; the formerly missing implication is proved for Banach spaces ([closed range and Fredholm criterion](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/Fredholm/ClosedRange.lean)). The Riesz–Schauder theory of \(1-K\), for a compact operator \(K\) on a Banach space, is proved: the kernel is finite-dimensional, the range is closed, and the cokernel is finite-dimensional, by Riesz's argument on the chain of ranges of the powers of \(1-K\) ([Riesz theory](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/RieszTheory.lean)). Hence \(1-K\) is Fredholm of index zero, and adding a compact operator to a Fredholm operator preserves Fredholmness and the index; the finite-rank case is also stated separately ([compact perturbations](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/Fredholm/CompactPerturbation.lean), [finite-rank perturbations](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/Fredholm/FiniteRank.lean)). On the Hilbert-space side, the Fredholm alternative is proved for compact perturbations of a coercive bilinear form, and the existence-and-uniqueness form of Lax–Milgram is packaged for that use ([coercive forms](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/Variational/Fredholm.lean), [Lax–Milgram](https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/blob/6e53de0d3ce9ea24d7d487e656ac4590b0c45b3a/TauCeti/Analysis/InnerProductSpace/LaxMilgram.lean)).
