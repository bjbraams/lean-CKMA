## Seminorms and local convexity

A set is balanced when scaling by every scalar of norm at most \(1\) leaves it invariant, and absorbent when every point lies in all sufficiently large scalings. Every neighbourhood of the origin in a topological vector space is absorbent. Over a nontrivially normed field, an absorbent submodule is the whole space, and a linear map is surjective if and only if its image contains an absorbent set. The balanced core is the largest balanced subset, and the balanced hull is the union of the scalings by scalars of norm at most \(1\). If the origin lies in the set, the balanced core is the intersection of the scalings by scalars of norm at least \(1\). In a topological vector space, the balanced neighbourhoods of the origin form a neighbourhood basis; if the space is regular, the closed balanced neighbourhoods do (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Basic.lean`, `Analysis/LocallyConvex/BalancedCoreHull.lean`).

A space is locally convex when the convex neighbourhoods of each point form a neighbourhood basis. In a topological module it is enough to check this at the origin. A real locally convex space is locally path-connected, and so is each of its convex subsets (`Topology/Algebra/Module/LocallyConvex.lean`).

`WithSeminorms` says that an existing topology is the topology generated by a family of seminorms: neighbourhoods of the origin are the finite suprema of open seminorm balls. The space is then a topological vector space over the scalar field. A family is separating if and only if the topology is \(T_1\). Convergence is seminormwise. If the family is countable and the scalar field is nontrivially normed, the topology is first countable. A normed space is the space of its norm seminorm. A semilinear map into a space whose topology is generated by seminorms is continuous if and only if each seminorm of the codomain remains continuous after composition, and a map bounded in the seminorm sense — each codomain seminorm estimated by a constant times a finite supremum of domain seminorms — is continuous. A continuous seminorm on such a space is estimated by finitely many of the generating seminorms. The property passes to induced topologies, subspaces, products, and infima of topologies (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/WithSeminorms.lean`).

A space is polynormable when its topology is generated by some family of seminorms, equivalently by all of its continuous seminorms. A topology generated by seminorms, with scalars normed over \(\mathbb{R}\), is locally convex over \(\mathbb{R}\). A polynormable space over \(\mathbb{R}\) is therefore locally convex. The file asks for the same conclusion over a general real or complex scalar field and does not prove it. In the other direction, a locally convex space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) is polynormable: the gauges of the absolutely convex open neighbourhoods of the origin are seminorms, and they generate the topology (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/AbsConvexOpen.lean`).

## Absolutely convex sets

A set is absolutely convex when it is convex and balanced. The absolutely convex hull is the smallest such set containing a given set; over a nontrivially normed ordered field it equals the convex hull of the balanced hull. The balanced hull of the convex hull is contained in the absolutely convex hull, and the file records that equality can fail. The closed absolutely convex hull is the smallest closed absolutely convex set containing the given set. In a topological vector space over a normed field, closure preserves absolute convexity, and the closed absolutely convex hull is the closure of the absolutely convex hull. In a locally convex space over a nontrivially normed ordered field, the absolutely convex neighbourhoods of the origin form a neighbourhood basis, and so do the open ones and the closed ones (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/AbsConvex.lean`).

## Polars

For a bilinear form \(B\) with values in a normed commutative ring, the absolute polar of a set \(s\) is the set of \(y\) such that \(\lVert B(x,y)\rVert\le 1\) for every \(x\in s\). The polar is an order-reversing Galois connection with the polar of the flipped form: the polar of a union is the intersection of the polars, the polar of the empty set is everything, and the polar of a singleton is a closed unit ball in the pairing. Every set is contained in its bipolar, and the tripolar equals the polar. The polar is closed in the weak topology induced by the flipped form. If the scalar field is nontrivially normed and \(B\) is right-separating, the polar of the whole space is \(\{0\}\). The polar of a set stable under scalar multiplication is the set of vectors that pair to zero with it, and is a submodule. The same definitions, applied to the duality pairing, give the polar in the continuous dual: the polar of the whole space is \(\{0\}\) (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Polar.lean`).

The bipolar theorem, identifying the bipolar with the closed absolutely convex hull, was not found. A comment in `Topology/Algebra/Module/IsWeak.lean` names that theorem as a reason for isolating the weak topology; it is not proved there either.

## Separation and the Hahn–Banach theorem

In a real topological vector space, if \(s\) is a convex open neighbourhood of the origin and \(x_0\notin s\), some continuous linear functional sends \(x_0\) to \(1\) and \(s\) into \((-\infty,1)\). Disjoint convex sets, one of them open, are weakly separated by a continuous linear functional. If both are open, the separation is strict. If one convex set has nonempty interior, disjoint from a second convex set, there is a weak separation of the two sets; when the second set is nonempty the functional may be taken nonzero. For \(x\) outside the interior of a convex set \(A\) with nonempty interior, a nonzero functional satisfies \(f(y)\le f(x)\) for all \(y\in A\); this is an attained maximum on \(A\) only if \(x\) belongs to \(A\). These statements do not use local convexity (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Separation.lean`).

Local convexity enters for compact and closed sets. In a real locally convex space, disjoint convex sets, one compact and one closed, are strictly separated. A point outside a closed convex set is strictly separated from it, and in a \(T_1\) space any two distinct points are strictly separated. A closed convex set is the intersection of the closed half-spaces containing it. If the space is hereditarily Lindelöf, countably many half-spaces suffice. The same statements hold for a continuous linear functional over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), with the real part in place of the functional (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Separation.lean`).

The analytic theorem is stated for polynormable spaces. Over \(\mathbb{R}\), a linear functional on a subspace dominated by a continuous seminorm extends to a continuous linear functional dominated by the same seminorm. Over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), a linear functional dominated in norm by a continuous seminorm extends to an element of the continuous dual with the same norm bound, and every continuous linear functional on a subspace extends to the whole space. A continuous linear map from a subspace into a Hausdorff topological vector space, with finite-dimensional range, extends, and a finite-dimensional subspace is closed and complemented (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/HahnBanach.lean`).

A space has separating dual when for every nonzero vector some continuous linear functional is nonzero on it. A \(T_1\) real locally convex space has separating dual, by the point-separation form of Hahn–Banach, and so does every normed space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\). Points are then separated by the dual, and the space is \(T_1\) or \(T_2\) when the scalar field is. On a finite-dimensional domain, linear functionals extend to continuous linear functionals on the whole space precisely when the inclusion of the domain is injective. The continuous linear equivalences act transitively on the nonzero vectors. If the scalar field is a central algebra over a commutative semiring, the continuous linear endomorphisms have scalar centre (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/SeparatingDual.lean`).

## Weak topology, strong topology, and boundedness

The weak topology induced by a bilinear pairing is the topology of the seminorms \(x\mapsto\lVert B(x,y)\rVert\). It is locally convex over \(\mathbb{R}\). A linear functional is continuous for the topology induced by a family of linear functionals if and only if it lies in their span. Every continuous linear functional on the weak space is evaluation against some vector of the pairing, and if the pairing is right-separating this is a linear equivalence between that space and the continuous dual of the weak space (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/WeakDual.lean`).

If two locally convex spaces over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) have the same continuous linear functionals, in the sense that a linear isomorphism identifies their continuous duals, then the isomorphism preserves closures of convex sets. In particular, in a locally convex space the closure of a convex set coincides with its weak closure, and the same is true of the closed convex hull. If the dual separates points, the weak space is Hausdorff (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/WeakSpace.lean`).

Banach–Alaoglu is not proved in this directory. For a normed space, and a scalar field that is a proper metric space, closed dual balls are weak-star compact, and so is the polar of a neighbourhood of the origin. If the space is separable, those sets are sequentially compact (`Analysis/Normed/Module/WeakDual.lean`).

A set is von Neumann bounded when every neighbourhood of the origin absorbs it. Compact sets are von Neumann bounded, and so are totally bounded sets in a uniform additive group with continuous scalar multiplication. The image of a von Neumann bounded set under a continuous linear map is von Neumann bounded. For a sequence of scalars tending to zero but not equal to zero, a set \(S\) is von Neumann bounded if and only if \(\varepsilon_n x_n\to 0\) whenever \(x_n\in S\). In a normed space over a nontrivially normed field, von Neumann boundedness coincides with metric boundedness. In a space whose topology is generated by seminorms, a set is von Neumann bounded if and only if each seminorm is bounded on it. A space is called quasi-complete when every closed von Neumann bounded set is complete; every complete space is quasi-complete, and in a quasi-complete uniform additive group with continuous scalar multiplication the closure of a totally bounded set is compact (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Bounded.lean`, `Analysis/LocallyConvex/WithSeminorms.lean`).

If the domain is first countable, a linear map that sends von Neumann bounded sets to von Neumann bounded sets is continuous (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/ContinuousOfBounded.lean`). The file presents this as the fact needed for bornological spaces. No definition of a bornological space was found.

The topology of uniform convergence on the von Neumann bounded sets, the strong topology on continuous linear maps, is locally convex when the codomain is. The same holds for uniform convergence on any directed family of sets. The topology of pointwise convergence is locally convex when the codomain is. When the codomain is normed, pointwise convergence is the seminorm topology \(A\mapsto\lVert Ax\rVert\), so a net converges if and only if it converges at each vector (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/StrongTopology.lean`, `Analysis/LocallyConvex/PointwiseConvergence.lean`).

The weak operator topology is the topology of pointwise convergence against the dual of the codomain, generated by the seminorms \(A\mapsto\lVert y(Ax)\rVert\). It is locally convex over \(\mathbb{R}\), coarser than the topology of bounded convergence, and Hausdorff when the codomain has separating dual. Composition is separately continuous, so the endomorphisms form a semitopological ring (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/WeakOperatorTopology.lean`).

## Barrelled spaces and Montel spaces

A space is barrelled when every lower semicontinuous seminorm is continuous. This is not the definition by barrels. The file records that barrels are not defined, and that the equivalence of the two definitions in the locally convex case is not proved. Any topological vector space over a nontrivially normed field which is a Baire space is barrelled. The file records that this includes Banach spaces and Fréchet spaces (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Barrelled.lean`).

Banach–Steinhaus, or the uniform boundedness principle: a pointwise bounded family of continuous semilinear maps from a barrelled space into a space whose topology is generated by a family of seminorms is uniformly equicontinuous. Pointwise bounded means that each seminorm of the codomain is bounded above on the orbit of each vector. If the codomain is only assumed polynormable, the same conclusion holds when each orbit is von Neumann bounded. A pointwise limit of continuous semilinear maps from a barrelled space into a Hausdorff polynormable space, taken along a countably generated nontrivial filter, is continuous and semilinear (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Barrelled.lean`).

A Montel space, in this directory, is a space with the Heine–Borel property: every closed von Neumann bounded set is compact. The file does not require the space to be barrelled, and it says that the usual definition is the conjunction of the two. This is not Montel's theorem on normal families of holomorphic functions. A normed Montel space over a complete nontrivially normed field is finite-dimensional. If the domain is a \(T_1\) Montel space, the topology of bounded convergence on its continuous linear maps coincides with the topology of compact convergence (`Analysis/LocallyConvex/Montel.lean`).

## Projective tensor seminorms

For a finite family of normed spaces, `Analysis/Normed/Module/PiTensorProduct/ProjectiveSeminorm.lean` puts the projective seminorm on their algebraic tensor product:
\[
\pi(u)=\inf_{u=\sum_j\bigotimes_i x_{ji}}\sum_j\prod_i\|x_{ji}\|.
\]
Continuous multilinear maps correspond isometrically to continuous linear maps on this seminormed tensor product (`PiTensorProduct.liftIsometry`). The canonical tensor map is continuous, and tensoring a family of bounded maps gives a bounded map whose norm is at most the product of their norms. Nondegeneracy and equality with the product norm on pure tensors remain TODOs in this file. This is a substantial tensor-product construction, but does not yet supply the completed projective/injective tensor-product theory used in nuclearity. The neighboring `InjectiveSeminorm.lean` is deprecated and explicitly says that its previous formalization was mathematically incorrect; it should not be counted as that theory.

## Test functions and distributions

A test function of order \(n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\) on an open set \(\Omega\) in a real normed space is a \(C^n\) map, with values in a real normed space, whose support is compact and contained in \(\Omega\). For each compact \(K\), the functions supported in \(K\) carry the topology of uniform convergence of derivatives up to order \(n\), described by the corresponding supremum seminorms, and that topology is locally convex. The space of all test functions carries the finest locally convex topology coarser than the inductive-limit topology of these inclusions. A linear map from the test functions into a locally convex space is continuous if and only if its restriction to the functions supported in each compact \(K\subset\Omega\) is continuous. Each inclusion is continuous. The file says that the inclusion is a topological embedding, and that this has not been proved (`Analysis/Distribution/ContDiffMapSupportedIn.lean`, `Analysis/Distribution/TestFunction.lean`).

The file introduces an \(F\)-valued distribution on \(\Omega\) as a continuous real-linear map from the real test functions on \(\Omega\) into \(F\), with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets of test functions. The introductory text takes \(E\) finite-dimensional and \(F\) locally convex. Neither hypothesis is part of the type: \(E\) is a real normed space and \(F\) is a real topological vector space. Finite-dimensionality is used for the identification of a distribution given by a function, and for the support of a Dirac mass. Testing is against real-valued test functions. A distribution with values in \(\mathbb{C}\) is the complex case, as a real vector space of values. There is a separate space of distributions of order at most \(n\). The file says that, for smooth test functions, compact convergence coincides with bounded convergence because the test-function space is Montel. That identification is a citation, not a theorem of this checkout (`Analysis/Distribution/Distribution.lean`).

A continuous linear map on the value space pushes distributions forward. The Dirac mass at \(x\) evaluates test functions at \(x\), and is zero when \(x\notin\Omega\). The directional derivative is defined by \(\langle\partial_v T,\varphi\rangle=\langle T,-\partial_v\varphi\rangle\). As a map from distributions of order at most \(k\) to distributions of order at most \(n\), it is the zero map unless \(k+1\le n\). On distributions of infinite order it is a continuous endomorphism, linear in the direction (`Analysis/Distribution/Distribution.lean`).

A locally integrable function \(f\), against a measure, induces the distribution \(\varphi\mapsto\int \varphi\,f\). The construction is zero when \(f\) is not locally integrable on \(\Omega\), and it respects equality almost everywhere, addition, and scalar multiplication. If \(E\) is finite-dimensional and Borel and the value space is a complete normed space, two locally integrable functions that induce the same distribution agree almost everywhere on \(\Omega\). The underlying fact is not special to distributions: a locally integrable function on a finite-dimensional real vector space, or on a \(\sigma\)-compact finite-dimensional real manifold, whose integral against every smooth compactly supported scalar test function vanishes, vanishes almost everywhere. The same statement localizes to an open set (`Analysis/Distribution/AEEqOfIntegralContDiff.lean`, `Analysis/Distribution/Distribution.lean`).

The support of a distribution is the intersection of the closed sets on whose complement the distribution vanishes, where vanishing on a set means annihilation of every test function supported there. The support is closed, and its complement is the largest open set of vanishing. The support of a directional derivative, or of an iterated directional derivative, is contained in the support of the distribution. If \(E\) is finite-dimensional and \(x\in\Omega\), the support of the Dirac mass at \(x\) is \(\{x\}\) (`Analysis/Distribution/Support.lean`).

No space of distributions on a manifold was found. The manifold statement above is only the almost-everywhere conclusion.

## Schwartz space

A Schwartz function between real normed spaces is a smooth function such that for all natural numbers \(k\) and \(n\), \(\lVert x\rVert^k\,\lVert D^n f(x)\rVert\) is bounded. The topology is the locally convex topology generated by the countable family of seminorms given by the best such bounds, indexed by pairs of natural numbers. The space is a first countable locally convex topological vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\). Completeness is not proved, so the space is not shown to be Fréchet, and it is not shown to be Montel. A smooth compactly supported function is a Schwartz function (`Analysis/Distribution/SchwartzSpace/Basic.lean`).

A function has temperate growth when it is smooth and every iterated derivative is bounded by a constant times \((1+\lVert x\rVert)^k\) for some \(k\). A measure has temperate growth when \((1+\lVert x\rVert)^{-n}\) is integrable for some \(n\). Finite measures have temperate growth, and so does Haar measure on a finite-dimensional space. Schwartz functions have temperate growth. Against a temperate measure on a second countable Borel space they are integrable, the integral is a continuous linear map on Schwartz space, and for \(p\ge 1\) the inclusion into \(L^p\) is continuous. If the domain is finite-dimensional and the measure is finite on compact sets, the image is dense in \(L^p\) for \(1\le p<\infty\) (`Analysis/Distribution/TemperateGrowth.lean`, `Analysis/Distribution/SchwartzSpace/Basic.lean`).

Differentiation is continuous on Schwartz space, both as a Fréchet derivative and as a directional derivative, and the Laplacian is the sum of the second directional derivatives along any orthonormal basis. For Haar measure on a finite-dimensional space, integration by parts holds for the directional derivative, with a change of sign, and for the Laplacian, without one (`Analysis/Distribution/SchwartzSpace/Deriv.lean`).

On a finite-dimensional real inner product space, the Fourier transform of a Schwartz function is a Schwartz function, and the transform is a continuous linear map. If the value space is complete, Fourier inversion holds, and the transform is a continuous linear equivalence, with inverse the inverse Fourier transform. The directional derivative of the transform is the transform of multiplication by \(-2\pi i\langle\cdot,m\rangle\), and the transform of a directional derivative is multiplication of the transform by \(2\pi i\langle\cdot,m\rangle\). The transform is self-adjoint for a continuous bilinear pairing: \(\int M(\mathcal{F}f,g)=\int M(f,\mathcal{F}g)\). Plancherel for Schwartz functions with values in a complete complex inner product space says
\[
\int\langle\mathcal{F}f,\mathcal{F}g\rangle=\int\langle f,g\rangle,
\]
and in particular the \(L^2\) norms agree. Pointwise, \(\lVert\mathcal{F}f(x)\rVert\le\lVert f\rVert_{L^1}\) (`Analysis/Distribution/SchwartzSpace/Fourier.lean`). The unitary extension to all of \(L^2\) is constructed in `Analysis/Fourier/LpSpace.lean`; see Section 27.

## Tempered distributions

A tempered distribution with values in a complex topological vector space \(F\) is a continuous complex-linear map from complex Schwartz functions into \(F\). The topology used here is pointwise convergence, not the strong dual topology. The file says that the comparison between pointwise convergence and the strong dual of a Fréchet–Montel space is missing, and that the pointwise topology is not barrelled, so uniform boundedness does not apply (`Analysis/Distribution/TemperedDistribution.lean`).

A measure of temperate growth is a tempered distribution by integration. A function of temperate growth, and a Schwartz function, induce tempered distributions by \(\varphi\mapsto\int\varphi\,f\). For \(p\ge 1\), an \(L^p\) function does as well, and the inclusion is continuous. If the domain is finite-dimensional and the measure is locally finite, that inclusion is injective. The distributional Fourier transform agrees, on Schwartz functions with complete normed values, with the Fourier transform of the function (`Analysis/Distribution/TemperedDistribution.lean`).

Directional derivatives and the Laplacian act continuously on tempered distributions, by transposition. Multiplication by a function of temperate growth is a continuous endomorphism; without temperate growth the operation is defined to be zero. On a finite-dimensional real inner product space, the Fourier transform of a tempered distribution is defined by \(\langle\mathcal{F}T,g\rangle=\langle T,\mathcal{F}g\rangle\). It is a continuous linear equivalence, inverted by the inverse transform. It intertwines directional derivatives with multiplication by \(\pm 2\pi i\langle\cdot,m\rangle\), as in the Schwartz case. The Fourier transform of the Dirac mass at the origin is the tempered distribution given by Lebesgue measure. The support of a tempered distribution is defined as for ordinary distributions. Multiplication by a temperate function and differentiation do not enlarge it, and the support of the Dirac mass at \(x\) is \(\{x\}\) when the domain is finite-dimensional (`Analysis/Distribution/TemperedDistribution.lean`, `Analysis/Distribution/Support.lean`).

A Fourier multiplier by a scalar function \(g\) is the continuous linear map \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\circ(g\cdot)\circ\mathcal{F}\) on Schwartz functions and on tempered distributions. Unless \(g\) has temperate growth, the multiplication step is zero. Multipliers compose by pointwise multiplication of the symbols. The directional derivative is \(2\pi i\) times the multiplier by \(x\mapsto\langle x,m\rangle\), and the Laplacian is \(-(2\pi)^2\) times the multiplier by \(x\mapsto\lVert x\rVert^2\). These cover translation-invariant special cases of pseudodifferential operators when the symbols satisfy the appropriate estimates, but no general symbol-class or quantization calculus is developed (`Analysis/Distribution/FourierMultiplier.lean`).
